# Self-determination theory

Edward Deci and Richard Ryan's self-determination theory (SDT) is the
best-supported theory of human motivation available, built on decades of
experimental work. Its central claim: people have three innate psychological
needs, and satisfying them produces autonomous motivation, wellbeing, and
persistence.

The three needs:

1. **Autonomy** — experiencing one's actions as self-endorsed and volitional.
   Not independence, and not the absence of structure. A person can be autonomous
   while following a rule they endorse.
2. **Competence** — experiencing oneself as effective, making progress, growing.
3. **Relatedness** — feeling connected to and cared about by others; belonging.

**Evidence: Strong.** One of the most extensively tested theories in
psychology, with several decades of experimental and field research across
cultures and domains, including work, health care, education, and sport. The
undermining effect of expected contingent rewards on intrinsic motivation is
meta-analytically robust and replicated, with the well-established boundary
conditions noted in this file. The strongest live debate is over how universal
the autonomy need is across cultures, where the evidence still favors
universality.

Use when: designing roles, reward systems, performance management, or explaining
why a well-paid team is disengaged.

Do not use when: the problem is a genuine capability gap or a structural
blockage. Motivation is over-diagnosed.


## The motivation continuum

SDT rejects the intrinsic/extrinsic binary in favor of a continuum of
internalization:

| Type | Description | Durability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Amotivation | No intention to act | — |
| External regulation | Reward or punishment | Lasts only while the contingency does |
| Introjected | Guilt, shame, ego, "I should" | Fragile, stressful |
| Identified | "This matters to me" | Robust |
| Integrated | "This is who I am" | Very robust |
| Intrinsic | Inherent enjoyment | Very robust |

The practical implication: the goal is not to make work intrinsically enjoyable,
which is often impossible. It is to move regulation up the continuum toward
identified and integrated — by explaining rationale, acknowledging that the task
is unpleasant, and offering choice about how it is done.


## The undermining effect

Robustly replicated: **tangible, expected, contingent rewards reduce intrinsic
motivation for tasks that were already intrinsically interesting.** They shift
regulation toward external and narrow attention to the rewarded metric.

Nuances that matter in practice:

* The effect applies to interesting tasks. For genuinely dull, algorithmic work,
  contingent pay works fine.
* Unexpected rewards and verbal recognition do not undermine, and can support
  competence — provided the praise is informational ("here is what you did that
  worked") rather than controlling ("good, keep doing that for me").
* Pay itself is not the problem. Underpayment is a powerful demotivator; the
  finding concerns *contingent* reward for interesting work, not fair salary.
  Deci's practical advice: pay people well and fairly, then take money off the
  table as a lever.


## Designing for the three needs

**Autonomy support**
* Provide meaningful rationale for requests, especially unwelcome ones.
* Offer choice where real choice exists; do not fake it.
* Acknowledge negative feelings rather than arguing with them.
* Minimize controlling language: "you must", "you have to", "I need you to".

**Competence support**
* Optimal challenge — slightly beyond current ability.
* Fast, specific, informational feedback.
* Visible progress; make improvement observable.
* Remove obstacles that make good work impossible; nothing destroys competence
  like being prevented from doing a job properly.

**Relatedness support**
* Stable teams; churn is a relatedness tax.
* Time for non-transactional interaction.
* Managers who show genuine regard for the person.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: burnout research maps closely onto SDT. Clinicians rarely
  lack meaning; they lack autonomy (protocols, targets, and administrative
  burden imposed without rationale or consultation) and competence satisfaction
  (prevented from delivering the standard of care they were trained for). Pizza
  and resilience training address neither.

* **Software**: autonomy over *how* is the single strongest engineering
  retention factor in most surveys. Competence is damaged by long feedback loops
  — a two-hour build denies the sense of effectiveness dozens of times a day.
  See [dora-metrics](../dora-metrics/).

* **Executive**: individual bonus schemes tied to narrow metrics predictably
  produce narrow behavior and undermine collaboration. If the work is complex
  and interdependent, contingent individual reward is working against the design
  of the organization.


## Limitations

* Needs are claimed as universal; cross-cultural strength of the autonomy need
  is debated, though evidence for universality is reasonably good.
* Much of the underlying research is laboratory-based with student samples.
* Does not address structure, strategy, or power.
