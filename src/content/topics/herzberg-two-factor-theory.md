# Herzberg two-factor theory

Frederick Herzberg's motivation-hygiene theory (1959) proposes that job
satisfaction and job dissatisfaction are **not opposite ends of one scale** but
two separate scales with different causes.

* **Hygiene factors** (extrinsic): their absence causes dissatisfaction; their
  presence merely removes dissatisfaction. Salary, company policy, supervision,
  working conditions, job security, status, relationships with peers.
* **Motivators** (intrinsic): their presence causes satisfaction and motivation.
  Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement,
  growth.

The practical consequence: **fixing hygiene factors does not motivate anyone.**
It moves people from dissatisfied to neutral. Motivation requires the other
scale.

**Evidence: Mixed, tending weak.** The specific two-factor structure is
probably a method artifact: it was derived from the critical incident
technique, in which people attribute good outcomes to themselves and bad
outcomes to circumstances, and studies using other methods often fail to
replicate the clean separation. The sample was engineers and accountants in
1950s Pittsburgh. What survives is the practical distinction between removing
pain and creating meaning, which is better grounded by
[self-determination-theory](../self-determination-theory/) and
[job-characteristics-model](../job-characteristics-model/).

Use when: an organization is repeatedly investing in perks, pay, and facilities
and getting no engagement improvement.

Do not use when: hygiene is genuinely broken. You cannot motivate your way past
unsafe staffing, broken equipment, or below-market pay.


## The four combinations

| | High hygiene | Low hygiene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High motivators** | Ideal: engaged and content | Motivated but complaining; will leave for a better package |
| **Low motivators** | Comfortable and bored; the "golden cage"; high tenure, low output | Worst case: disengaged and dissatisfied; expect exit |

The "comfortable and bored" cell is the one organizations most often create by
accident, by responding to every engagement survey with hygiene investment.


## Job enrichment vs. job enlargement

Herzberg's prescription was **job enrichment**: adding responsibility, autonomy,
and scope for achievement — a *vertical* addition. He distinguished this sharply
from **job enlargement**: adding more tasks of the same kind, which is
horizontal and adds nothing.

His memorable formulation: **KITA** ("kick in the ass") produces movement, not
motivation. The person moves because you pushed. Stop pushing and movement
stops. Motivation is a self-charging generator.


## The criticisms

* **Method-bound**: derived from the critical incident technique, in which
  people describe good and bad episodes. People attribute good outcomes to
  themselves (motivators) and bad outcomes to circumstances (hygiene) — a
  self-serving bias that could produce the two-factor pattern artifactually.
  Studies using other methods often fail to replicate the clean separation.
* **Sample**: engineers and accountants in 1950s Pittsburgh.
* **Individual differences ignored**: the same factor is a motivator for one
  person and hygiene for another.
* **Salary is ambiguous**: Herzberg classified it as hygiene, but it also
  functions as recognition and status.

Evidence: mixed. The specific two-factor structure is not well supported; the
practical distinction between "removing pain" and "creating meaning" remains
useful, and is better grounded by
[self-determination-theory](../self-determination-theory/) and the
[job-characteristics-model](../job-characteristics-model/).


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: staff retention initiatives concentrating on parking, meals,
  and wellbeing apps are hygiene. They matter — genuinely broken hygiene drives
  people out — but they do not address the motivator side: autonomy over
  practice, opportunity to develop, and recognition of clinical achievement.

* **Software**: free food, good hardware, and competitive salary are hygiene.
  Interesting problems, ownership of a system, and visible impact are motivators.
  Firms that compete only on hygiene experience high turnover to firms that
  offer the second column, at similar pay.

* **Executive**: use the two lists to audit last year's people investment. If
  more than about half went to the hygiene column, that explains the flat
  engagement trend.
