# Weisbord six-box model

Marvin Weisbord's 1976 six-box model is the fastest useful organizational
diagnostic. Six boxes, one question each, plus an environment ring around them.

**Evidence: Weak.** Never validated as a measurement model, and never tested for
predictive value. It is a facilitation device: six prompts that reliably
generate a useful conversation in ninety minutes. The one component with
independent support is the formal/informal distinction, which mirrors the
well-documented espoused-theory versus theory-in-use gap. See
[double-loop-learning](../double-loop-learning/).

Use when: you need a shared diagnosis in one meeting, with a group that has no
appetite for a twelve-factor causal model.

Do not use when: you need to establish cause and effect, or the problem is
mainly about external adaptation. Use
[burke-litwin-causal-model](../burke-litwin-causal-model/) instead.


## The six boxes

1. **Purpose**: what business are we in? Do people agree, and does the agreement
   hold under pressure?
2. **Structure**: how do we divide the work? Does the structure fit the purpose?
3. **Relationships**: how do we manage conflict between people, between units,
   and between people and their technology?
4. **Rewards**: is there an incentive for doing all that needs doing? What is
   actually rewarded, as opposed to stated?
5. **Leadership**: does someone keep the boxes in balance, and hold the
   organization's purpose visible?
6. **Helpful mechanisms**: do we have adequate coordinating technologies —
   planning, budgeting, measurement, information systems, meetings?

**Environment**: the ring around the six. What demands do outside forces make?


## The two diagnoses per box

Weisbord's key move: for each box, examine both

* the **formal system** — what the documents say, and
* the **informal system** — what actually happens.

The gap between them is the diagnosis. A team that has a documented escalation
path nobody uses has a Relationships and Leadership problem, not a documentation
problem.


## The "fit" questions

* Fit between organization and environment: can we meet outside demands?
* Fit between individual and organization: do people find it satisfying to work
  here, or do they comply because they must?


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: Purpose is usually strong — clinicians agree on patient care.
  Rewards is usually the weak box: activity targets and throughput metrics push
  against the stated purpose, and clinicians feel the contradiction daily.

* **Software**: Helpful mechanisms is the box most often mistaken for the
  problem ("we need a better tracker"), while Structure and Relationships are
  the real gaps. Ask what the tracker is being asked to compensate for.

* **Executive**: Leadership box asks whether anyone is actively holding the
  balance. In many executive teams every member owns a box and nobody owns the
  fit between them.


## Running it

1. Ninety minutes, mixed-level group of eight to twelve.
2. One flip chart per box; formal on the left, informal on the right.
3. Silent writing first, then round-robin. Prevents the loudest voice anchoring.
4. Dot-vote the single box with the biggest formal/informal gap.
5. Ask "what would we have to believe for this gap to be intentional?"


## Limitations

* No causal ordering; it will not tell you which box to fix first.
* Six boxes is a simplification, deliberately.
* Dated language: "helpful mechanisms" needs translation for modern audiences.
