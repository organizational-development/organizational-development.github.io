# Employee engagement surveys

Engagement surveys are the most widely deployed OD instrument and among the most
poorly used. This file covers what they can and cannot tell you, and how to run
one that produces change rather than a deck.

**Evidence: Mixed.** The academic work engagement construct, measured by the
UWES, has sound psychometrics and meta-analytic associations with performance
and wellbeing. The practitioner engagement bundle is conceptually muddy and
overlaps heavily with older, better-defined constructs.
Engagement-to-performance correlations are real but modest, and causality runs
in both directions. eNPS as a single item is noisy and heavily influenced by
recent events; it is the weakest instrument in common use.

Use when: you have a genuine intent and the authority to act on the results, and
a plan for feeding data back locally. See
[survey-feedback-method](../survey-feedback-method/).

Do not use when: you cannot act. An unactioned survey lowers trust below where
it started.


## What "engagement" means

The construct is contested. Common definitions:

* **Work engagement** (Schaufeli, Bakker): vigor, dedication, absorption. The
  academic construct, measured by the UWES scale. Reasonable evidence base.
* **Employee engagement** (practitioner/consultancy): a bundle of satisfaction,
  commitment, discretionary effort, and advocacy. Commercially useful, and
  conceptually muddy — it overlaps heavily with job satisfaction and
  organizational commitment, which are older and better-defined constructs.

Be explicit about which you mean. "Engagement went up two points" is
uninterpretable without knowing what was measured.


## Common instruments

* **Gallup Q12** — twelve items, strong practitioner validation, focused on the
  manager's local unit. Note its design: nearly every item is actionable by a
  frontline manager, which is deliberate.
* **UWES** — academic work engagement scale.
* **eNPS** — "how likely are you to recommend this as a place to work?"
  Single item; cheap, noisy, heavily influenced by recent events, and
  frequently over-interpreted.
* **Custom** — usually a mixture, often with poor psychometrics, often too long.


## Design rules

* **Short.** Twelve to thirty items. Long surveys reduce response rate and
  response quality, and generate data nobody analyzes.
* **Actionable items only.** Never ask about something you will not or cannot
  change.
* **Stable core plus rotating module.** A fixed core lets you track trend; a
  rotating set explores this year's question.
* **Free text, sparingly.** Comments are the most valuable and most sensitive
  data. Say exactly who will read them, verbatim, before collecting.
* **Segment carefully.** Meaningful cuts, with a minimum reporting threshold
  (commonly 5–10) held firmly. Small-team demographic cuts re-identify people.
* **Cadence.** Annual is too slow to steer and too infrequent to act on;
  continuous pulsing produces fatigue and drifting comparability. Quarterly
  short pulses with an annual deeper survey is a reasonable default.


## Reading the results

* **Trend beats absolute.** A score of 6.8 means little; 6.8 falling from 7.4
  means something.
* **Variance beats mean.** The organizational average hides the fact that team A
  is at 8.5 and team B is at 4.2. The variance is the actionable finding, and it
  is what averaging destroys.
* **Manager effect dominates.** Local management explains far more variance than
  enterprise policy, which is why local feedback and local action matter more
  than the corporate program.
* **Response rate is data.** A falling response rate is a result in itself,
  usually meaning the last round produced nothing.
* **Beware the honeymoon and the hangover.** New leaders score high early;
  restructures depress scores for months regardless of anything else.


## The action gap

The single largest failure. Surveys are run, results are presented, and nothing
observable changes. Countermeasures:

* Each team picks **one** thing, not five, and does it within 90 days.
* Publish "you said, we did" — including honest refusals with reasons.
* Escalate only what teams genuinely cannot control, and answer those publicly.
* Report the action-completion rate alongside the scores, at board level. This
  changes behavior more than the scores do.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: national staff surveys are large, mandatory, slow, and
  frequently disconnected from local action. Their value is realized only at
  ward and department level, and only when the local manager works the local
  data with their own team.

* **Software**: pair engagement with flow and developer-experience measures.
  Engagement that is high while lead time is terrible usually means a pleasant
  team tolerating a bad system — and those people leave later, not never. See
  [space-framework](../space-framework/).

* **Executive**: resist single-number governance. Ask instead for the
  distribution across teams, the action-completion rate, and the free-text
  themes that leadership found uncomfortable. That last request tells the
  organization more than the survey does.


## Ethics

* Genuine anonymity, genuinely explained, and honored — including when a senior
  person asks who wrote a comment.
* Never use results for individual performance management.
* Publish the analysis method and the reporting threshold.
* Know your regulatory regime and any works council or union obligations before
  collecting. See [od-ethics](../od-ethics/).
