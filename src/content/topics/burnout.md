# Burnout

Christina Maslach's definition treats burnout as a syndrome arising from
**chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed**, with three
dimensions: exhaustion, cynicism or depersonalization, and reduced professional
efficacy.

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 classifies burnout as an occupational
phenomenon rather than a medical condition, and specifies that the term applies
to the work context and should not describe experiences in other areas of life.

The central claim, and the one most often reversed in practice: **burnout is a
property of the job, not of the person.**

**Evidence: Good.** Three decades of research with a validated instrument and
consistent findings across occupations and countries; the three-dimensional
structure replicates well. Two genuine controversies remain. The boundary
between burnout and depression is contested and imperfectly separated by current
measures. And there is no agreed clinical cut-off score, so prevalence figures
vary enormously between studies depending on the threshold chosen — treat any
specific "X% of clinicians are burned out" claim with suspicion.

Use when: exhaustion, disengagement, or turnover is rising and you want to
locate the organizational cause rather than treat symptoms.

Do not use when: as a synonym for tiredness, or as an individual diagnosis. It
is a work-context syndrome, and treating it as a personal resilience deficit is
both unsupported and the most common failure described below.


## The six areas of worklife

Maslach and Leiter's model is the actionable part, because each area names a
**job condition** that can be changed. Burnout arises from mismatch between the
person and the job in one or more of:

1. **Workload** — too much work, too little time, insufficient resources to do
   it properly. The most obvious and rarely the only one.
2. **Control** — little autonomy over how the work is done, or responsibility
   without the authority to discharge it.
3. **Reward** — insufficient recognition, financial or social. Absence of
   acknowledgement, more than absence of pay.
4. **Community** — isolation, unresolved conflict, absence of supportive
   relationships at work.
5. **Fairness** — inequity in workload, pay, or promotion; disrespect;
   procedures that are not transparently applied.
6. **Values** — conflict between what the person believes the work is for and
   what the organization requires. **The strongest single predictor in much of
   the research**, and the one wellbeing programmes never address.

Diagnose by asking which areas are mismatched, then change the job condition. An
intervention that does not change any of the six is not a burnout intervention.


## Why individual interventions underperform

Reviews of burnout interventions consistently find that organization-directed
interventions — workload redesign, schedule change, increased participation in
decisions — outperform person-directed ones such as resilience training,
mindfulness, and stress management. Person-directed interventions produce real
but small and short-lived effects.

The mechanism is straightforward: if the cause is a job condition, teaching the
person to tolerate it does not remove it, and being offered tolerance training
in place of a fix is itself a fairness and values mismatch. This is why
wellbeing programmes are frequently received with anger rather than gratitude,
which surprises the people who commissioned them.

The honest position: individual support is worth providing and is not a
substitute for changing the work.


## On measurement

The Maslach Burnout Inventory is the standard instrument and is **copyrighted
and licensed**. It is not reproduced here, and it should not be reproduced in
your internal survey without a licence. Freely usable alternatives exist,
including the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory and the Oldenburg Burnout Inventory.

For most OD purposes, the six areas of worklife are the more useful thing to
measure anyway, because each maps to an intervention. A burnout score tells you
there is a problem; an area-of-worklife profile tells you which job condition to
change.

Watch the same trap as elsewhere: a rising willingness to report exhaustion can
follow improved psychological safety. See
[measurement-and-metrics](../measurement-and-metrics/).


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: the most-studied population, and the one where the individual
  framing does most damage. Clinicians rarely have a values mismatch about
  patient care; they have a values mismatch between the care they were trained
  to give and the care the system permits, plus workload and control mismatches
  from documentation burden and rota design. Resilience training offered to a
  clinician who cannot reliably take a break reads as an insult, and is
  interpreted as the organization declining to fix the job.

* **Software**: on-call load is a workload and control mismatch simultaneously —
  the hours are unpredictable and the engineer cannot influence when they occur.
  Long feedback loops damage professional efficacy, the third dimension, by
  making it hard to feel effective. Fixing the deployment pipeline is a burnout
  intervention, though nobody labels it as one. See
  [dora-metrics](../dora-metrics/), [space-framework](../space-framework/).

* **Executive**: executives experience burnout through the values and fairness
  areas more than workload, which they usually chose. The specific executive
  risk is being the person who must implement decisions they disagree with. Note
  also that an executive's own hours set the workload norm for everyone below,
  regardless of stated policy. See
  [mckinsey-influence-model](../mckinsey-influence-model/) on role modelling.


## Limitations

* The burnout–depression boundary is genuinely unclear, and some researchers
  argue the constructs overlap substantially.
* No agreed clinical threshold, so prevalence claims are not comparable across
  studies.
* Most research is cross-sectional and self-report, so causal direction between
  job conditions and burnout is inferred rather than demonstrated.
* The syndrome is defined for the work context; applying it to life generally is
  outside what the evidence supports.
