# Psychological Safety Questionnaire

To measure psychological safety in a team, use this questionnaire. Part A is Amy
Edmondson's seven-item scale, the standard instrument. Part B places the team on
the safety-and-accountability grid, because safety without standards is the
failure mode critics attack. Part C records behavioral indicators, which are
harder to fake than a survey.

See [psychological-safety](../psychological-safety/).

**Evidence: Strong.** The underlying construct has meta-analytic support across
three decades, linking it to learning behavior, error reporting, information
sharing, innovation, and performance. The seven-item scale is the most widely
validated instrument here. Two honest caveats carry into practice: it is
self-report, and the construct is team-level, so an organization-wide average
hides the variance that actually matters.


## Before you run it

* **Team level only.** Psychological safety varies sharply between teams in the
  same organization — which is good news, because a local leader can change it
  without waiting for the enterprise. An organizational average destroys the
  finding.
* **Never report individual responses.** Reporting them destroys the thing being
  measured, immediately and durably.
* **Minimum five responses** before reporting any result.
* **Do not run it if you cannot act.** Asking people whether it is safe to speak
  up, and then doing nothing, is itself evidence that it is not.
* Say who will see the results, in what form, before anyone answers.


## Part A: The seven-item scale

Rate each 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

**Items 1, 3, and 5 are reverse-scored**: subtract the response from 8 before
averaging. They are worded negatively on purpose, to interrupt straight-lining.

1. If you make a mistake on this team, it is often held against you.
   *(reverse-scored)*
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

3. People on this team sometimes reject others for being different.
   *(reverse-scored)*
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.

   efforts.
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

## Part B: The safety and accountability grid

Psychological safety is not comfort, and safety without standards produces
pleasant teams that achieve nothing. Score both axes.

**Safety** — use the Part A mean.

**Accountability** — rate 1 (none) to 7 (high):

1. This team has clear, demanding standards for the quality of its work.
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]
2. People here are expected to deliver what they committed to.
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]
3. Underperformance is addressed rather than absorbed.
   - Rating (1–7): [ ]

**Accountability mean:** [ ]

Place the team:

| | Low accountability | High accountability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High safety** | Comfort zone | **Learning and high performance** |
| **Low safety** | Apathy zone | Anxiety zone |

Most organizations that believe they have a safety problem are in the **anxiety
zone**: standards without safety. Most teams that have invested in safety alone
land in the **comfort zone**, which is what critics of the concept are pointing
at when they call it soft.

## Analysis

* **Read the distribution, not the mean.** A team mean of 5.2 made of responses
  ranging 2 to 7 is a different problem from one made of responses clustered at
  5. Wide spread usually means safety is conditional — safe for some people, or
     on some topics, and the pattern of who is excluded is the finding.

* **Compare items 1 and 2 against 6 and 7.** Items 1–5 measure whether risk is
  punished; 6 and 7 measure whether contribution is valued. A team can be
  non-punitive and still ignore people. The remedy differs.

* **Part C item 1 is usually the most startling number available.** Measure it
  once. Where the most senior person holds more than half the talking time, the
  survey scores are optimistic regardless of what they say.

* **Part C item 5 is the definitive test.** One challenge that was punished
  teaches the whole team more than a year of survey improvement. If the
  consequence column contains anything negative, treat the Part A score as
  unreliable — people are answering the way it is safe to answer.

* **Beware the ceiling.** Teams frequently score 5.5 to 6 on first
  administration because they have nothing to compare against and because
  answering honestly is itself an interpersonal risk. Trend across
  administrations is more informative than the first absolute value.

* **If accountability is low, do not invest further in safety.** Add clarity,
  standards, and follow-through instead. See
  [google-project-aristotle](../google-project-aristotle/), where dependability
  was the second-ranked factor and the one most often neglected.

* **Expect reporting to rise as safety improves.** Incident reports, near
  misses, escalated concerns, and raised risks all increase when people become
  willing. This looks like deterioration and is success. Agree the
  interpretation with leadership before the numbers move. See
  [measurement-and-metrics](../measurement-and-metrics/).

* **The leader is the intervention.** Where scores are low, the highest-leverage
  change is the leader's response to the next piece of unwelcome news, not a
  team workshop. See
  [westrum-organizational-culture-typology-questionnaire](../westrum-organizational-culture-typology-questionnaire/)
  for the organizational counterpart, and [just-culture](../just-culture/) for
  what must happen after failure.
