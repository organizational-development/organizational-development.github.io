# World café

World café, developed by Juanita Brown and David Isaacs, is a structured
conversational process for large groups. Participants sit at small café-style
tables, discuss a question in rounds, and rotate between rounds so ideas
cross-pollinate.

It is the easiest large-group method to run well, and the one most often run as
decoration.

**Evidence: Weak.** No controlled evidence. The small-group-then-rotate
structure has a plausible mechanism, since small groups reliably increase the
proportion of people who speak, and that specific effect is supported by group
dynamics research. Everything else — the number of rounds, the table hosts,
the tablecloths — is convention. The value is almost entirely in question
design and harvesting, neither of which the method itself guarantees.

Use when: you want broad input and shared understanding across a large group;
when you want people to hear each other rather than hear a presenter.

Do not use when: you need a decision, a prioritized list, or accountability.
World café generates collective insight, not commitments.


## Structure

* Tables of four to five. Four is better than six; five is the ceiling for
  everyone speaking.
* Paper tablecloths and pens. Writing and drawing while talking is part of the
  method, not a nicety — the artifacts carry ideas between rounds.
* Three rounds of 20–30 minutes, each on a question that builds on the last.
* Between rounds, all but one person move to different tables. The **table host**
  stays, welcomes new arrivals, and summarizes what was discussed.
* A closing **harvest**: whole-group sharing of what emerged, captured visibly.


## The seven design principles

1. **Set the context.** Be clear about purpose and about what will happen with
   the output. Say honestly what is and is not in scope.
2. **Create hospitable space.** Physical environment shapes conversation
   quality. Round tables, refreshments, natural light if you can get it.
3. **Explore questions that matter.** The question determines the value of the
   event; everything else is logistics.
4. **Encourage everyone's contribution.** Small tables do most of this work
   automatically.
5. **Connect diverse perspectives.** The rotation is the mechanism.
6. **Listen together for patterns and insights.** Ask people to listen for what
   is emerging, not to prepare a rebuttal.
7. **Share collective discoveries.** The harvest, made visible.


## Question design

The most under-invested element. Good café questions are:

* Simple and clear.
* Open — no yes/no, no implied answer.
* Thought-provoking, and slightly uncomfortable.
* Relevant to the participants' actual experience and within their influence.
* Progressive across rounds.

A standard three-round progression:

1. **Discover the present.** "What is your experience of X today?"
2. **Deepen.** "What is making X the way it is? What is really going on?"
3. **Move to possibility.** "What would need to be true for X to be different?
   What is one thing you could do?"


## Harvesting

Where most cafés fail. Options, in increasing order of usefulness:

* Popcorn share-out — quick, shallow, and the default.
* Graphic recording on a shared wall — visible, memorable.
* Each table writes its three most important insights on cards; cards clustered
  live by the whole group.
* Post-event synthesis document circulated within 48 hours, with a named owner
  for each theme and a stated decision route.

If nobody can say what happens next, do not run the café.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: effective for whole-department or cross-organization
  conversations — for instance a café on "what would excellent discharge look
  like?" including ward staff, social care, therapists, and porters. Mixed
  tables across professions and grades produce information no single-profession
  meeting can.

* **Software**: useful at scale for topics like "what slows us down?" across
  many teams, where a single retrospective cannot reach. Follow with concrete
  ownership or engineers will correctly classify it as a talking shop.

* **Executive**: valuable for connecting an executive team to frontline
  experience at scale, provided executives sit at tables as participants and do
  not host. An executive at a table changes what is said; brief them to listen
  and to speak last.


## Limitations

* No decisions, no priorities, no accountability by design.
* Requires a genuine intent to use the output; performative cafés are
  transparent and increase cynicism.
* Rotation loses depth: a table just reaching a real insight is broken up.
* Introverts and juniors still speak less, though far more than in plenary.
