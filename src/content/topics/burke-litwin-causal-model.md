# Burke-Litwin Causal Model

The Burke-Litwin Causal Model of Organizational Performance and Change is a system-based diagnostic framework created by W. Warner Burke and George H. Litwin in 1992. Unlike static checklists, it maps explicit cause-and-effect relationships among twelve specific organizational dimensions to explain how external forces cascade through an enterprise to influence individual motivation and final performance. [1, 2, 3] 
**Evidence: Mixed.** More empirically ambitious than most diagnostics here,
and only partly validated. The transformational versus transactional
distinction is the best-supported element: it corresponds to a real and
repeatedly observed failure pattern, in which structural or procedural fixes
are applied to problems rooted in leadership, strategy, or culture. The full
twelve-factor causal structure — including the specific direction and strength
of each path — has not been confirmed, and some published tests find the
hypothesized causal ordering only partially supported. Treat the tiers as
sound and the individual arrows as hypotheses.

Use when: performance is declining and you need the root cause rather than the
symptom; during deep cultural change; when previous fixes have not worked and
nobody can say why.

Do not use when: you need a diagnosis this week. The model is data-heavy and
requires significant stakeholder consultation across twelve interacting factors.
For a rapid screen, use [weisbord-six-box-model](../weisbord-six-box-model/) or
[mckinsey-7s-framework](../mckinsey-7s-framework/) first, then come here when a
specific breakdown is flagged.


## The Input, Throughput, and Output Architecture
The model is structured hierarchically, showing that changes at the top or outer rings exert a dominant causal flow downward into internal operations and individual behaviors. [2] 

* Input (1 Factor):
1. External Environment: Market conditions, competitors, regulations, technologies, and economic factors outside the organization that act as the ultimate trigger for adaptation. [1] 
* Throughput - Transformational Factors (3 Factors):
These deep-seated levers alter the fundamental character and identity of the enterprise (transformational change).
2. Mission and Strategy: What the organization aims to achieve and its fundamental approach to competing.
3. Leadership: The executive behaviors and role-modeling that set the tone and direction for the system.
4. Organizational Culture: The shared values, unwritten rules, norms, and core beliefs that define "the way we do things here". [1, 3, 4, 5] 
* Throughput - Transactional Factors (7 Factors):
These everyday operational mechanisms respond to the transformational tier and drive incremental alignment (transactional change).
5. Structure: The formal hierarchy, reporting lines, and allocation of decision rights.
6. Management Practices: How managers execute strategy through prioritization, planning, and resource deployment.
7. Systems (Policies & Procedures): The formal routines, IT tools, and standard operating processes.
8. Work Unit Climate: The localized team atmosphere, trust levels, and psychological safety.
9. Task Requirements & Individual Skills: The technical and interpersonal capabilities required versus the actual talent profile.
10. Individual Needs & Values: What specific employees care about (autonomy, recognition, purpose) relative to rewards.
11. Motivation: The psychological engine and energy level that converts workplace experience into effort. [3, 6] 
* Output (1 Factor):
12. Individual and Organizational Performance: The final result (productivity, revenue, retention, customer satisfaction) that feeds back up to influence the external environment. [3, 7] 

## Core Mechanics and Change Logic

* Transformational vs. Transactional: A key utility of the model is telling leaders when they face a transformational challenge (requiring shifts in strategy, leadership, or culture) versus a transactional challenge (tweaking structure, systems, or climate). Trying to fix a cultural or strategic misfit with a surface-level restructuring will stall out. [6] 
* Motivation as a Mediator: Employee motivation sits near the bottom as a central clearinghouse. How workers experience the upstream structure, climate, and management practices directly dictates their motivation, which dictates performance. [3]
