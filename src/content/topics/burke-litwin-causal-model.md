# Burke-Litwin Causal Model

The Burke-Litwin Causal Model of Organizational Performance and Change is a
system-based diagnostic framework created by W. Warner Burke and George H.
Litwin in 1992. Unlike static checklists, it maps explicit cause-and-effect
relationships among twelve specific organizational dimensions to explain how
external forces cascade through an enterprise to influence individual motivation
and final performance. [1, 2, 3]

**Evidence: Mixed.** More empirically ambitious than most diagnostics here, and
only partly validated. The transformational versus transactional distinction is
the best-supported element: it corresponds to a real and repeatedly observed
failure pattern, in which structural or procedural fixes are applied to problems
rooted in leadership, strategy, or culture. The full twelve-factor causal
structure — including the specific direction and strength of each path — has not
been confirmed, and some published tests find the hypothesized causal ordering
only partially supported. Treat the tiers as sound and the individual arrows as
hypotheses.

Use when: performance is declining and you need the root cause rather than the
symptom; during deep cultural change; when previous fixes have not worked and
nobody can say why.

Do not use when: you need a diagnosis this week. The model is data-heavy and
requires significant stakeholder consultation across twelve interacting factors.
For a rapid screen, use [weisbord-six-box-model](../weisbord-six-box-model/) or
[mckinsey-7s-framework](../mckinsey-7s-framework/) first, then come here when a
specific breakdown is flagged.


## The Input, Throughput, and Output Architecture
The model is structured hierarchically, showing that changes at the top or outer
rings exert a dominant causal flow downward into internal operations and
individual behaviors. [2]

* Input (1 Factor):
1. External Environment: Market conditions, competitors, regulations,
   technologies, and economic factors outside the organization that act as the
   ultimate trigger for adaptation. [1]
* Throughput - Transformational Factors (3 Factors): These deep-seated levers
  alter the fundamental character and identity of the enterprise
  (transformational change).
2. Mission and Strategy: What the organization aims to achieve and its
   fundamental approach to competing.
3. Leadership: The executive behaviors and role-modeling that set the tone and
   direction for the system.
4. Organizational Culture: The shared values, unwritten rules, norms, and core
   beliefs that define "the way we do things here". [1, 3, 4, 5]
* Throughput - Transactional Factors (7 Factors): These everyday operational
  mechanisms respond to the transformational tier and drive incremental
  alignment (transactional change).
5. Structure: The formal hierarchy, reporting lines, and allocation of decision
   rights.
6. Management Practices: How managers execute strategy through prioritization,
   planning, and resource deployment.
7. Systems (Policies & Procedures): The formal routines, IT tools, and standard
   operating processes.
8. Work Unit Climate: The localized team atmosphere, trust levels, and
   psychological safety.
9. Task Requirements & Individual Skills: The technical and interpersonal
   capabilities required versus the actual talent profile.
10. Individual Needs & Values: What specific employees care about (autonomy,
    recognition, purpose) relative to rewards.
11. Motivation: The psychological engine and energy level that converts
    workplace experience into effort. [3, 6]
* Output (1 Factor):
12. Individual and Organizational Performance: The final result (productivity,
    revenue, retention, customer satisfaction) that feeds back up to influence
    the external environment. [3, 7]

## Core Mechanics and Change Logic

* Transformational vs. Transactional: A key utility of the model is telling
  leaders when they face a transformational challenge (requiring shifts in
  strategy, leadership, or culture) versus a transactional challenge (tweaking
  structure, systems, or climate). Trying to fix a cultural or strategic misfit
  with a surface-level restructuring will stall out. [6]
* Motivation as a Mediator: Employee motivation sits near the bottom as a
  central clearinghouse. How workers experience the upstream structure, climate,
  and management practices directly dictates their motivation, which dictates
  performance. [3]

## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: falling performance on a pathway, traced upstream, typically
  lands at management practices and work unit climate — not at individual
  skills, which is where a retraining programme would have been aimed. The model
  earns its cost here by preventing an expensive intervention at the wrong tier.

* **Software**: a delivery slowdown traced upstream usually lands at structure
  and systems — coupling, approval gates, shared pipelines — not at motivation,
  which is where an engagement programme would have been aimed. See
  [conways-law](../conways-law/) and [dora-metrics](../dora-metrics/).

* **Executive**: the model's chief executive value is preventing you from
  authorizing a restructure to solve a leadership problem, including when the
  leadership problem is your own. That is also why executives find it
  uncomfortable, and why the contracting conversation in
  [entry-and-contracting](../entry-and-contracting/) matters before you start.


## Limitations

* Data-heavy and slow: twelve interacting factors require real stakeholder
  consultation, not a workshop.
* More than most groups will hold in mind at once; the tiers are easier to teach
  than the individual paths.
* The causal arrows are the least validated part. Treat the transformational and
  transactional tiers as sound, and any specific path as a hypothesis.
