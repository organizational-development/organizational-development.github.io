# Schein model of organizational culture

Edgar Schein defined organizational culture as:

> A pattern of shared basic assumptions that the group learned as it solved its
> problems of external adaptation and internal integration, that has worked well
> enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as
> the correct way to perceive, think, and feel in relation to those problems.

Two things follow. Culture is **learned solutions that worked**, which is why it
is defended: it is the accumulated answer to real past problems. And culture
operates mostly **below awareness**, which is why asking people to describe
their culture yields the wrong layer.

**Evidence: Moderate.** The three-level structure is a widely accepted
conceptual framework rather than a measurement model, and the inference of
underlying assumptions from artifacts is interpretive by design. The
leader-mechanism claims — that what leaders attend to, how they respond to
crises, and who they promote shape culture more than formal statements do —
have partial support from leadership and socialization research. Conceptually
strong, operationally soft.

Use when: a change keeps failing for reasons nobody can articulate; when stated
values and actual behavior diverge; when integrating two organizations.

Do not use when: the problem is structural. Culture is over-diagnosed because it
is unfalsifiable and blames nobody in particular.


## The three levels

1. **Artifacts** — visible, easy to observe, hard to interpret. Office layout,
   dress, rituals, language, tools, meeting behavior, stories, what hangs on the
   wall. You can catalog artifacts in a day and misread every one of them.

2. **Espoused beliefs and values** — the stated strategies, goals, and
   philosophies. The values page, the mission statement, what leaders say. These
   describe what the group says it wants, which may or may not match behavior.

3. **Basic underlying assumptions** — unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs
   that actually determine behavior. Non-negotiable in the sense that
   contradicting them produces confusion or anger rather than debate. This is
   the culture.

The diagnostic work is inferring level 3 from level 1, and treating the gap
between levels 2 and 3 as the finding.


## Reading the third level

Underlying assumptions are inferred, never asked directly. Useful probes:

* What happens when someone makes a mistake here? Watch, do not ask.
* What gets someone promoted? Compare with what the values page says.
* What can be said in a meeting, and what is only said afterwards in the
  corridor?
* Which rules are broken routinely and without consequence?
* Who is a hero here, and what story is told about them?
* What surprised you in your first month? Ask new joiners in week three, before
  they acclimatize. This is the single best culture instrument available and it
  costs nothing.


## Changing culture

Schein's position: culture cannot be changed directly. It changes when the group
learns that a new way solves its problems better. Levers, in rough order of
power:

1. **What leaders pay attention to, measure, and control** — routinely.
2. **How leaders react to critical incidents and crises.** One layoff, one
   handled failure, one tolerated bad actor teaches more than a year of values
   work.
3. **How leaders allocate resources** and rewards.
4. **Deliberate role modeling and coaching.**
5. **Criteria for recruitment, promotion, and exit** — the strongest long-run
   lever, because it changes who "we" are.
6. Secondary: structure, systems, rituals, physical space, stories, formal
   statements. Formal values statements are the *weakest* mechanism, and the one
   most organizations start with.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: espoused value "speak up for safety"; underlying assumption
  "the consultant is not questioned." The artifact that reveals it: who speaks
  first on a ward round, and who never speaks. See [just-culture](../just-culture/),
  [crew-resource-management](../crew-resource-management/).

* **Software**: espoused "we value quality"; underlying assumption "shipping on
  the announced date is what actually counts." The artifact: what happens in the
  last week before a release, every time.

* **Executive**: subculture conflict is the executive's real culture problem.
  Schein noted the persistent clash between the *operator* culture (the people
  doing the work), the *engineering* culture (designers of systems, who prefer
  people-free solutions), and the *executive* culture (financial abstraction,
  necessarily impersonal at scale). Most enterprise dysfunction is this
  three-way misunderstanding, not a missing values statement.


## Limitations

* Hard to measure; qualitative and interpretive.
* Slow to change — years, not quarters.
* "Culture" is used as a residual explanation for anything unexplained. Rule
  it in only after structure, incentives, and workload have been excluded.
