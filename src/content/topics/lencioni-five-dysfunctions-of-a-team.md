# Lencioni five dysfunctions of a team

Patrick Lencioni's *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2002) presents a pyramid
of five stacked dysfunctions, each enabling the one above it. It is widely used
with leadership teams because it is memorable and it names the avoidance
patterns executives recognize in themselves.

**Evidence: Weak.** A business fable, not research: no validation, no sample,
no measurement. It remains popular because executives recognize themselves in
it, which is a fact about resonance rather than about accuracy. Its first
level overlaps heavily with [psychological-safety](../psychological-safety/),
which covers similar ground with far better evidence, and the commitment
principle — people commit to decisions they disagree with if they were
genuinely heard — is consistent with procedural justice research.

Use when: working with an intact leadership team that is polite, busy, and not
achieving much together.

Do not use when: the problem is structural, or the "team" is actually a
collection of individuals with no shared work. Many executive groups are the
latter, and no amount of trust work makes them a team.


## The pyramid, bottom up

1. **Absence of trust.** Specifically *vulnerability-based* trust: the
   willingness to say "I was wrong", "I need help", "I don't know". Its absence
   produces invulnerability — people manage impressions instead of working.

2. **Fear of conflict.** Without trust, teams create artificial harmony. Real
   disagreement moves to corridors and side channels. The tell is the meeting
   that ends with agreement and is relitigated by email that evening.

3. **Lack of commitment.** Without open conflict, people do not buy in. They
   comply. Lencioni's key claim: **people will commit to a decision they
   disagree with if they believe they were genuinely heard.** Consensus is not
   required; being heard is.

4. **Avoidance of accountability.** Without commitment, peers will not challenge
   each other's behavior or performance. Everything routes to the leader, who
   becomes the sole source of accountability and therefore the bottleneck.

5. **Inattention to results.** Without peer accountability, individuals pursue
   their own unit's status, budget, or ego over the collective result. This is
   the visible symptom that usually prompts the call for help.


## The diagnostic sequence

Work bottom up. Attacking inattention to results directly — with dashboards,
targets, and exhortation — fails when the cause is three levels below. Ask:

* Do people admit mistakes in front of each other? (1)
* Does the team have real, unscripted arguments about important things? (2)
* Can everyone state the last three decisions and why they were made? (3)
* Does anyone challenge a peer without going to the boss first? (4)
* Do people prioritize their function over the shared goal? (5)


## Interventions per level

| Dysfunction | Practical moves |
| --- | --- |
| Trust | Personal histories exercise; leader goes first with a real admission; behavioral profiles used for understanding, not labelling |
| Conflict | Name conflict as a norm; assign a devil's advocate; "mining" for buried disagreement; explicitly ask the quiet person |
| Commitment | Cascading messaging at the end of every meeting: what we decided, what we tell our teams; deadlines forced; worst-case scenario analysis |
| Accountability | Publish goals and standards; regular peer progress reviews; team rewards rather than only individual ones |
| Results | A single, visible scoreboard of collective outcomes; reward team results |


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: a hospital executive team where each director defends their
  directorate's budget is at level 5, caused by level 2 — nobody will say aloud
  that one service is over-resourced relative to need. The finance meeting
  cannot fix that.

* **Software**: an engineering leadership group that agrees on a technical
  direction in the meeting and whose teams then continue as before is at level
  3. The decision was never actually made; it was announced into silence.

* **Executive**: Lencioni's most useful question for a CEO — "which team is your
  first team: the one you lead, or the one you're a member of?" Executives who
  answer "the one I lead" are describing level 5, and the answer is usually
  honest and usually the problem.


## Limitations

* No empirical validation; a business fable, not research. See
  [evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).
* Overlaps heavily with [psychological-safety](../psychological-safety/), which
  has far better evidence for the same first level.
* Assumes a real team with genuine interdependence. Applying it to a group that
  merely reports to the same person produces frustration.
* The vulnerability exercises can be intrusive and coercive if run badly,
  particularly where there is a real power gradient in the room. Participation
  must be genuinely optional. See [od-ethics](../od-ethics/).
