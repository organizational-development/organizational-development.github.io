# Galbraith Star Model Questionnaire

To run a star model design diagnostic, use this questionnaire. It assesses the
five design policy areas and their alignment, from 1 (badly misaligned) to
5 (fully aligned).

Use it before a reorganization, not after. Its main practical purpose is to
prevent a structure-only change — the most common and most expensive design
failure.

See [galbraith-star-model](../galbraith-star-model/).

**Evidence: Moderate.** The underlying information-processing theory has support;
the five-point framework is a design heuristic and has not been tested as a
model.


## Step 1: Strategy

1. Clarity

- Question: Is the strategy specific enough to serve as a design criterion —
  that is, could two people use it to choose between two structures and reach
  the same answer?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

2. Choices

- Question: Does the strategy say what we will *not* do, and what we are willing
  to be worse at?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

3. Design criteria

- Question: Have we written three to five explicit criteria that any design must
  satisfy?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]


## Step 2: Structure

1. Specialization

- Question: Do we have the right kinds of specialists for this strategy, at the
  right depth?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

2. Shape

- Question: Are spans of control and the number of layers appropriate — neither
  starving managers of attention nor inserting layers that only relay?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

3. Distribution of power

- Question: Are decisions made at the level with the relevant information, and is
  centralization vs. decentralization a deliberate choice rather than an
  accident?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

4. Departmentalization

- Question: Is it clear which dimension has primacy — function, product,
  geography, or customer — and does everyone give the same answer?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]


## Step 3: Processes

1. Vertical processes

- Question: Do planning, budgeting, and resource allocation actually direct
  resources toward the strategy, on a cadence that matches the work?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

2. Lateral processes

- Question: Do we have explicit mechanisms — integrator roles, standing forums,
  shared metrics, defined teams — for coordinating across units without
  escalating?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

3. Escalation load

- Question: How often do cross-unit conflicts reach a common boss for
  resolution? (Score 5 for rarely, 1 for constantly.)
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]


## Step 4: Rewards

1. Goals and metrics

- Question: Do the metrics people are judged on match the behavior the strategy
  requires?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

2. Compensation and recognition

- Question: Does pay, bonus, and recognition reinforce the strategy, or reward
  the previous one?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

3. Promotion criteria

- Question: Do the people who get promoted here demonstrate the behavior the new
  design requires?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]


## Step 5: People

1. Capability

- Question: Do we have the skills the design assumes exist, particularly in the
  roles the design newly creates?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

2. Development

- Question: Do we have a route to build those capabilities on the timescale the
  design needs?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]

3. Selection and rotation

- Question: Do recruitment and movement of people support the design, or does
  everyone stay in one silo for a career?
  - Rating (1–5): [ ]


## Step 6: Analysis

* **The lowest-scoring point is the binding constraint**, regardless of how much
  effort has gone into the others. A star with four strong points and one weak
  point behaves like the weak point.

* **Check Rewards specifically.** Rewards is the point most commonly left
  untouched during a redesign, and it silently vetoes the other four. If
  Step 4 scores below 3 while Structure scores above 4, expect the
  reorganization to fail and to be blamed on culture.

* **Low Step 3 lateral process scores with high Structure scores** mean you have
  drawn boxes and not designed coordination. This is the cheapest thing to fix:
  a named integrator role or a standing cross-unit forum frequently resolves
  what looks like a structural problem, at a fraction of the cost.

* **A low score on Step 2, item 4 — dimension primacy — predicts constant
  escalation.** If the leadership team cannot agree which dimension wins, the
  organization has a matrix in which every conflict routes to the top.

* **Low Step 1 scores mean stop.** Design arguments without strategy criteria are
  unresolvable, and the loudest or most senior person will win by default.

* **Name what will get worse.** Every design accepts a tradeoff. Before
  proceeding, write down what this design is deliberately choosing to be worse
  at. Unnamed downsides become surprises, and surprises become evidence that the
  design failed.
