# Nadler-Tushman congruence model

David Nadler and Michael Tushman's congruence model (1980) treats the
organization as an open system that transforms inputs into outputs, and locates
performance in the **fit** between four internal components rather than in the
quality of any one of them.

The central claim: the greater the congruence among components, the higher the
performance. A world-class component that does not fit the others reduces
effectiveness.

**Evidence: Moderate.** The specific model has not been validated as an
instrument, but its central principle is grounded in contingency theory, which
has substantial support: there is no single best organizational form, and
effectiveness depends on fit between the organization and its task and
environment. Person-organization fit research supports the People pairings
specifically. Stronger than most diagnostics here, and still not a measurement
tool.

Use when: performance is poor despite good people, good strategy, and good tools
— that is, when the problem is fit rather than quality.

Do not use when: you need a quick shared vocabulary. Use
[weisbord-six-box-model](../weisbord-six-box-model/).


## Structure

**Inputs**

* Environment: markets, regulation, competitors, technology.
* Resources: people, capital, technology, brand.
* History: precedents, past decisions, scars.
* Strategy: the choices made about where and how to compete. Nadler and Tushman
  treat strategy as an input to the transformation process, not as part of it.

**Transformation process — the four components**

1. **Work**: the actual tasks, their flow, their inherent uncertainty and
   interdependence.
2. **People**: skills, expectations, preferences, demographics.
3. **Formal organization**: structure, processes, systems, metrics, rewards.
4. **Informal organization**: culture, norms, networks, actual power, unwritten
   rules.

**Outputs**: at system, unit, and individual level.

**Feedback**: outputs loop back to inputs.


## The six fit pairs

Diagnosis means examining all six pairwise fits:

| Fit | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Work ↔ People | Do people have the skills the work demands, and does the work meet their needs? |
| Work ↔ Formal | Do structures and processes support the work, or obstruct it? |
| Work ↔ Informal | Does the culture help get the work done? |
| People ↔ Formal | Do structures meet people's needs; are they used as intended? |
| People ↔ Informal | Does the culture fit the people's values? |
| Formal ↔ Informal | Do the stated system and the real system agree? |

The last pair is usually where the diagnosis lands.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: Work is high-uncertainty and highly interdependent. Formal
  organization is often designed for standardized throughput. That single misfit
  explains a large share of clinician frustration and workaround behavior.

* **Software**: an org adopts autonomous product teams (Formal) while retaining
  centralized annual funding and approval (Formal ↔ Work misfit). Autonomy is
  announced; dependency is preserved. Teams correctly conclude the change was
  cosmetic.

* **Executive**: a strategy shift toward long-horizon platform investment
  (Input) against a quarterly bonus system (Formal) and a hero culture that
  rewards firefighting (Informal). The strategy is not wrong; it is incongruent.


## Applying it

1. Establish outputs: what is actually underperforming, at which level.
2. Describe the work as it truly is, including its uncertainty and coupling.
3. Assess each of the four components.
4. Score all six fits; find the worst.
5. Intervene on the misfit, not on the component that is easiest to change.


## Limitations

* Requires more analytic effort than a six-box or 7S session.
* Congruence can mean comfortable stagnation; a perfectly congruent organization
  can be perfectly aligned to a market that no longer exists. Fit is necessary,
  not sufficient.
* Strategy sits outside the model, so it will not challenge the strategy.
