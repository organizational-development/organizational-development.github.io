# Rogers diffusion of innovations

Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962) explains how new ideas and
practices spread through a social system over time. It is among the
better-evidenced models in this collection, drawn from hundreds of empirical
diffusion studies.

**Evidence: Strong.** One of the best-evidenced models in this collection,
synthesized from hundreds of empirical diffusion studies across agriculture,
medicine, and communications, and extended by thousands since. The five
attributes that predict adoption rate — relative advantage, compatibility,
complexity, trialability, observability — are the most robust part and explain a
substantial share of variance in adoption. The adopter categories and their
percentages are a statistical convention rather than a law, and the categories
are retrospective rather than stable traits.

Use when: rolling out a practice across many teams, and deciding where to start
and how to sequence.

Do not use when: the change is mandatory and immediate, such as a regulatory
deadline. Diffusion describes voluntary adoption.


## Adopter categories

Proportions are from Rogers' aggregate findings, and vary by context.

| Category | Share | Characteristics | How to work with them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Innovators | ~2.5% | Venturesome, tolerate risk, often outside the mainstream | Give them early access; do not use them as proof |
| Early adopters | ~13.5% | Respected opinion leaders, judicious | **The critical group.** Their endorsement legitimizes |
| Early majority | ~34% | Deliberate, adopt just before average | Need evidence from peers, not vendors |
| Late majority | ~34% | Skeptical, adopt under peer pressure or necessity | Need the old option to be genuinely gone or clearly worse |
| Laggards | ~16% | Traditional, reference the past | Often hold valid knowledge about why the old way exists |

The label "laggard" is pejorative and frequently unfair. Late adopters
disproportionately include people with the most operational risk exposure and
the longest institutional memory. Interview them; they know the failure modes.


## The five attributes that predict adoption

The strongest practical part of the model. An innovation spreads faster when it
has:

1. **Relative advantage**: better than what it replaces, *in the adopter's own
   terms* — not the sponsor's.
2. **Compatibility**: fits existing values, experience, and needs.
3. **Complexity** (inverse): easy to understand and use.
4. **Trialability**: can be tried in a limited way, reversibly.
5. **Observability**: results are visible to others.

Rogers found these attributes explain a large share of the variance in adoption
rates. This makes them a design checklist: before rollout, score your change on
all five and fix the weakest.


## The chasm

Geoffrey Moore's addition: a gap between early adopters and early majority.
Early adopters accept incomplete solutions for advantage; the early majority
require a complete, low-risk, well-supported product. A rollout that succeeds
with enthusiasts and stalls at scale has hit the chasm — the answer is usually
completeness and support, not more evangelism.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: clinical practice change spreads through respected clinicians
  and specialty networks, not through management communication. Identify the
  early adopters with
  [organizational-network-analysis](../organizational-network-analysis/), and
  note that observability is often low in clinical work — make the results
  visible with local data.

* **Software**: internal platform adoption follows this precisely. Trialability
  (easy to try, easy to back out) and observability (public dashboards, visible
  early users) matter more than mandates. A mandated platform with poor relative
  advantage produces shadow systems.

* **Executive**: resist the instinct to mandate at 15% adoption. Mandating
  before the early majority has peer evidence converts adoption into compliance,
  and compliance does not produce the benefits the business case assumed.


## Limitations

* Pro-innovation bias: assumes adoption is good. Some innovations should not
  spread.
* Individual-blame bias: attributes non-adoption to the person rather than to
  the innovation or the system.
* Categories are retrospective and not stable traits; the same person is an
  early adopter of one thing and a laggard on another.
