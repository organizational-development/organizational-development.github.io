# Immunity to change

Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey's immunity to change (ITC) explains why people fail
to change behaviors they genuinely want to change. Their claim: the failure is
not weak willpower or insufficient knowledge. It is a **competing commitment**,
held with equal sincerity, protected by a **big assumption** that is usually
untested.

The metaphor is immunological: the system is not broken; it is actively and
skillfully protecting itself against something it perceives as a threat.

**Evidence: Weak.** Limited independent empirical validation; the supporting
evidence is largely case material produced by the developers and their
practitioners. The adult development theory underneath it is contested. The
method's practical defense is different and does not depend on the theory: it
converts vague complaints into specific, falsifiable claims about what actually
happens to people here, which can then be tested directly. Requires skilled
facilitation and genuine safety, and is intrusive when done badly.

Use when: an individual or group repeatedly fails at a change they sincerely
endorse; when "we know what to do and we don't do it".

Do not use when: the barrier is structural, capability-based, or a lack of
resources. ITC is for genuine self-contradiction, not for obstacles.


## The four-column map

**Column 1 — Improvement goal.** One important commitment, stated positively and
in the person's own words. "I am committed to delegating more."

**Column 2 — Doing / not doing.** Behaviors that work against column 1. Be
concrete and non-judgmental. "I take back tasks I've assigned. I rewrite my
team's drafts. I attend meetings I've delegated."

**Column 3 — Hidden competing commitments.** Ask: what would be the worst thing
about doing the opposite of column 2? Sit with the fear, then translate it into
a commitment. "I am also committed to never having my name on substandard work."
Both commitments are real. The person is not weak; they are perfectly designed
to produce the current result.

**Column 4 — Big assumptions.** The beliefs that make column 3 feel necessary.
"I assume that if I let something imperfect through, my reputation is
permanently damaged and I will lose my standing."

The big assumption is stated as fact and treated as fact, but it has usually
never been tested.


## Testing big assumptions

Not confrontation — experiment. Design a small, safe, reversible test:

* Modest in scope, low cost if the assumption turns out true.
* Data-generating: decide in advance what you will observe.
* Run it, record what actually happened, and compare with the prediction.

"Let one piece of my team's work go out unedited, and note what actually
happens." Most big assumptions do not survive three honest tests.


## Collective immunity

The map works at group level, and this is where OD uses it. An executive team's
column 1 might be "we are committed to faster decisions"; column 2, "we defer
every decision for more analysis"; column 3, "we are committed to never being
the one who was wrong"; column 4, "we assume that a visible mistake ends a
career here."

Column 4 is then a testable claim about the organization — and frequently a true
one, in which case the intervention is not psychological. It is changing what
actually happens to people who are wrong. See [just-culture](../just-culture/),
[westrum-organizational-culture-typology](../westrum-organizational-culture-typology/).

That is the highest-value use of ITC in OD: it converts vague cultural
complaints into specific, falsifiable claims about consequences.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: a consultant committed to developing juniors who never lets
  them lead. Competing commitment: never being responsible for a preventable
  harm. Big assumption: supervision at a distance equals negligence. Testable —
  in graduated, defined steps.

* **Software**: a team committed to continuous delivery that keeps a manual
  release gate. Competing commitment: never being the team that broke
  production. Big assumption: automated release means unrecoverable failure.
  Testable, with a feature flag and a rollback drill.

* **Executive**: a CEO committed to empowering the executive team who reviews
  every decision. Competing commitment: never being surprised in a board
  meeting. Big assumption: any surprise reads as loss of control. Testable by
  agreeing a class of decisions the CEO explicitly will not see.


## Limitations

* Time-intensive; a proper map takes hours, and group work takes days.
* Requires real psychological safety and skilled facilitation. Poorly done, it
  is intrusive and can be coercive — participation must be genuinely optional.
  See [od-ethics](../od-ethics/).
* Limited independent empirical validation.
* Risks psychologizing structural problems. Always rule out obstacles first.
