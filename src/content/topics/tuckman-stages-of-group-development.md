# Tuckman stages of group development

Bruce Tuckman's 1965 model: **forming, storming, norming, performing**, with
**adjourning** added in 1977. It is the most widely taught team model in
existence and one of the least empirically supported.

**Evidence: Weak.** A 1965 literature review of roughly 50 studies, mostly of
therapy and training groups rather than work teams. Later research does not find
that teams reliably pass through these stages in this order; Gersick's
punctuated equilibrium work suggests a quite different pattern, with an abrupt
transition near the temporal midpoint. The model also assumes stable membership,
which most modern teams do not have. One message survives: early conflict in a
new team is normal.

Use when: normalizing early team conflict so a new team does not conclude it is
broken; giving a leader vocabulary for what to expect.

Do not use when: predicting a team's trajectory, diagnosing a stuck team, or
justifying inaction ("we're just storming"). The evidence does not carry that
weight.


## The five stages

1. **Forming**: polite, tentative, dependent on the leader. Members test what is
   acceptable. Real disagreement is suppressed. Task progress is low; the work
   is orientation.

2. **Storming**: conflict over goals, roles, approach, and status. Frustration
   with the leader is common. Many teams stall here permanently, settling into a
   pseudo-norming truce in which the disagreement is simply no longer voiced.

3. **Norming**: agreement on how to work together. Norms form, roles settle,
   cohesion rises. Risk: cohesion purchased by avoiding the original conflict,
   which then resurfaces under pressure.

4. **Performing**: the team works interdependently on the task with little
   process overhead. Disagreement is about the work, not about the people.

5. **Adjourning** (or *mourning*): disbanding. Real loss for high-performing
   teams, and routinely unmanaged. See
   [bridges-transition-model](../bridges-transition-model/).


## The evidence problem

* Tuckman's model was a literature review of about 50 studies, mostly of therapy
  groups, not work teams.
* Later research does not find that teams reliably pass through these stages in
  this order. Connie Gersick's punctuated equilibrium findings suggest teams
  instead maintain an initial pattern until roughly the temporal midpoint of a
  project, then undergo a sharp transition.
* Modern teams rarely have the stable membership the model assumes; people join
  and leave continuously, and "the team" is a moving object.

Evidence: weak. See [evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).


## What to use it for anyway

The one genuinely useful message: **early conflict in a new team is normal and
is not evidence of a wrong hire or a bad team.** That message prevents real
damage, and it is worth the model's continued use.

Beyond that, prefer models with better support:

* [psychological-safety](../psychological-safety/) — what actually predicts team
  learning.
* [google-project-aristotle](../google-project-aristotle/) — what predicted
  effectiveness in a large sample.
* [drexler-sibbet-team-performance-model](../drexler-sibbet-team-performance-model/)
  — more actionable, question-based, and diagnostic.
* [lencioni-five-dysfunctions-of-a-team](../lencioni-five-dysfunctions-of-a-team/)
  — also weakly evidenced, but more directly prescriptive.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: rotating junior staff mean many clinical teams re-form every
  few months and never leave forming. The remedy is not stage management; it is
  explicit, fast onboarding of norms — a written team charter, an introduction
  ritual, and a named person to ask.

* **Software**: a newly formed squad arguing about branching strategy in week
  two is storming, and is fine. A squad still arguing about it in month six has
  an unresolved decision-rights problem, not a stage problem.

* **Executive**: a new executive team's storming is expensive and public.
  Accelerate it deliberately with a facilitated chartering session — surface the
  conflicts on purpose rather than waiting for them to emerge in front of the
  organization.


## Practical alternative: charter instead of stage-watch

Rather than waiting for stages, run a chartering session in week one:

* Purpose: why does this team exist, and what happens if it does not?
* Success: how will we know, by when?
* Roles: who decides what, and who must be consulted?
* Norms: meetings, response times, disagreement, escalation.
* Review: when do we revisit this? Put the date in the calendar now.
