# Future search

Future search, developed by Marvin Weisbord and Sandra Janoff, is a structured
large-group planning meeting that brings the **whole system into the room** to
find common ground and produce action commitments.

Distinctive feature among large-group methods: it is tightly designed. Unlike
[open-space-technology](../open-space-technology/), the agenda is fixed; the
content is entirely the participants'.

**Evidence: Weak.** Case-based, largely documented by its developers, with no
controlled trials. Two design choices have independent support: getting the
whole system in the room reflects the boundary-spanning finding that cross-unit
contact reduces attribution error between groups, and the public admission step
draws on well-established reciprocity and intergroup contact research. The
three-day format and the five-task sequence are convention.

Use when: a complex issue crosses organizational boundaries, requires many
parties to act, and no single party can solve it alone.

Do not use when: one organization can simply decide; when the sponsor wants a
predetermined outcome; when key stakeholders will not attend.


## The four conditions

1. **Get the whole system in the room.** All stakeholders with Authority,
   Resources, Expertise, Information, and Need — the **ARE IN** mnemonic.
   Typically 60–80 people in eight mixed stakeholder groups.
2. **Explore the whole before seeking to fix parts.** Global context before
   local action.
3. **Focus on common ground and desired futures**, treating problems and
   conflicts as information rather than as items for action. Areas of
   disagreement are recorded and explicitly not worked on.
4. **Have people manage their own groups** and take responsibility for their own
   actions.


## The five tasks, over three days

Classically sixteen hours across three days, so that participants sleep twice —
the overnight processing is part of the design.

1. **Review the past.** Groups build timelines of the last few decades:
   personal, organizational, and global. Establishes shared history and shows
   that the present had causes.
2. **Explore the present — external trends.** Build a "mind map" of forces
   affecting the topic, then each stakeholder group identifies which trends it
   is acting on.
3. **Explore the present — owning our actions.** Each stakeholder group reports
   what it is proud of and what it is sorry about in relation to the topic. This
   session does the emotional work that makes day three possible, and it is the
   one sponsors most often want to cut.
4. **Create ideal future scenarios.** Mixed groups present a future as though it
   already exists, in whatever form they choose.
5. **Identify common ground and plan action.** Confirm shared ground publicly,
   record differences without debating them, then form voluntary action groups
   with named commitments and dates.


## Why "prouds and sorries" matters

Asking each stakeholder group to state publicly what it is not proud of, in
front of the others, changes the room. It converts an adversarial framing into a
shared one faster than any facilitation technique, because each party has
already conceded something. Skipping it — because it is uncomfortable and
consumes three hours — is the most common way to hollow out a future search.


## Managing conflict by not working it

The counterintuitive rule: differences are acknowledged, recorded on a flip
chart, and **not resolved**. Groups work only on what they agree about.

The rationale: common ground is nearly always larger than expected, and it is
sufficient to generate substantial action. Attempting to resolve deep conflict
in a three-day meeting consumes the whole meeting and resolves nothing. This
also means future search is not the right method when the conflict *is* the
issue.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: designing an integrated care pathway with hospital
  clinicians, primary care, social care, ambulance, commissioners, voluntary
  sector, patients, and carers in one room. No single organization controls the
  pathway, which is exactly the condition future search is built for.

* **Software**: useful for platform or standards decisions spanning many teams
  and business units, where a central mandate would be resisted and no team can
  act alone.

* **Executive**: for cross-sector or ecosystem issues — regional skills, supply
  chain resilience, industry standards — where the executive's own organization
  is one actor among many.


## Limitations

* Logistically demanding: three days, 60–80 people, senior time.
* Requires genuine stakeholder attendance; a missing party invalidates the
  premise.
* Skilled facilitation is essential; the design is unforgiving of improvisation.
* Action commitments are voluntary, so follow-through needs deliberate support
  in the weeks afterwards — schedule the first check-in before people leave.
