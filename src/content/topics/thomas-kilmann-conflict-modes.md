# Thomas-Kilmann conflict modes

Kenneth Thomas and Ralph Kilmann's model describes five conflict-handling modes,
positioned on two axes:

* **Assertiveness**: the extent to which you try to satisfy your own concerns.
* **Cooperativeness**: the extent to which you try to satisfy the other's
  concerns.

**Evidence: Weak instrument, useful taxonomy.** The five-mode scheme is widely
used and intuitively sound, but the TKI is a forced-choice ipsative
instrument, which limits its psychometric properties and makes between-person
comparison inappropriate. There is little evidence that mode preference is
stable or predictive. The better-supported complement is principled
negotiation's interests-versus-positions distinction, which has substantial
support in negotiation research.

Use when: a specific conflict is stuck, or when a team or leader habitually uses
one mode regardless of the situation.

Do not use when: labelling people. The modes are situational choices, not
personality types, despite how the instrument is often used.


## The five modes

| Mode | Assertive | Cooperative | Stance | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Competing** | High | Low | "My way" | Emergency; unpopular but necessary decisions; protecting against exploitation |
| **Accommodating** | Low | High | "Your way" | You are wrong; the issue matters far more to them; building goodwill for later |
| **Avoiding** | Low | Low | "No way, not now" | Trivial issue; no chance of winning; emotions too hot; more information needed |
| **Compromising** | Medium | Medium | "Halfway" | Equal power, moderate stakes, time pressure; temporary settlement |
| **Collaborating** | High | High | "Our way" | Both sets of concerns matter too much for compromise; need commitment; want to learn |


## The two most useful observations

**Collaborating is not always right.** It is expensive in time and energy. A
leader who collaborates on everything cannot decide, and a team that collaborates
on trivia exhausts itself. Match the mode to the stakes.

**Compromising is not collaborating.** Compromise splits the difference and
leaves both parties partly unsatisfied; it settles positions. Collaboration
digs beneath positions to interests and can produce an option neither party
brought. Most groups that believe they collaborated actually compromised, and
the tell is that no new option appeared.


## Diagnosing habitual overuse

| Overused mode | Visible cost | Underused signal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Competing | People stop bringing you problems; you are surrounded by agreement | Nobody argues with you any more |
| Accommodating | Your team's needs are consistently deprioritized; resentment | You cannot recall the last thing you refused |
| Avoiding | Decisions rot; the same issue returns quarterly | Long-standing issues nobody will name |
| Compromising | Solutions satisfy nobody; principles traded away | No creative options ever emerge |
| Collaborating | Everything takes forever; trivia gets full treatment | Simple decisions require a workshop |


## Interests, not positions

The complement to this model is principled negotiation (Fisher and Ury,
*Getting to Yes*):

* Separate the people from the problem.
* Focus on interests, not positions.
* Invent options for mutual gain before deciding.
* Insist on objective criteria.

Positions are what people say they want; interests are why. Two units fighting
over a headcount (positions) may have interests in delivery certainty and in
career progression, both satisfiable without the headcount.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: a surgeon and an anaesthetist disagreeing about proceeding
  should not compromise. This is a case for either collaboration, if time
  permits, or a clear pre-agreed decision rule if it does not. Splitting the
  difference on a clinical risk is the worst available option.

* **Software**: architecture disagreements are frequently avoided until they
  become expensive. Use a decision record with an explicit deadline to force the
  conflict into the open while it is still cheap. See
  <https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/decision-record>.

* **Executive**: executives commonly overuse compromising in resource
  allocation — every unit gets a proportional cut, nobody is happy, and the
  strategy is not served. Strategy requires competing choices, which means
  someone loses. An executive team that has never had a genuine loser has not
  made a strategic choice.


## Limitations

* The TKI instrument is forced-choice self-report; scores are best treated as
  conversation starters, not measurement.
* Says nothing about power asymmetry, which changes what modes are even
  available to a junior person.
* Risk of type-casting: "she's a competer" is a misuse.
