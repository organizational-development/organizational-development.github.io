# Hofstede cultural dimensions

Geert Hofstede's dimensions describe *national* cultural differences, derived
originally from IBM employee surveys across 40+ countries in the 1960s–70s.

**Evidence: Contested.** Enormously influential and seriously criticized. The
main methodological objections stand: a single company, a single era, a mostly
male professional sample, dimensions derived by factor analysis on limited
items, and nations treated as though they were cultures. The decisive
practical objection is the ecological fallacy — within-country variance
exceeds between-country variance on most dimensions, so applying a national
score to an individual is invalid. Use it to generate questions about
cross-border friction; never to characterize a person.

Use when: working across national boundaries — multinational teams, offshore
delivery, cross-border merger integration — as a source of hypotheses about
where friction may arise.

Do not use when: characterizing an individual, or explaining organizational
culture. Both are misuses, and both are extremely common.


## The six dimensions

1. **Power distance**: the extent to which less powerful members accept unequal
   power distribution. High: hierarchy expected, deference normal, disagreement
   with a superior is costly. Low: flatter expectations, challenge is acceptable.

2. **Individualism vs. collectivism**: ties to self and immediate family, versus
   integration into cohesive in-groups with mutual obligation.

3. **Motivation towards achievement and success** (originally
   "masculinity/femininity"): preference for competition, achievement, and
   assertiveness, versus cooperation, modesty, and quality of life. The original
   naming is widely criticized; the renamed version is now used.

4. **Uncertainty avoidance**: tolerance for ambiguity. High: need for rules,
   structure, precision, and planning; ambiguity is threatening. Low: comfort
   with improvisation and open-ended situations.

5. **Long-term orientation** (Confucian dynamism): future-oriented persistence
   and thrift, versus respect for tradition and near-term social obligation.

6. **Indulgence vs. restraint**: allowing gratification of natural human drives,
   versus regulating them through strict social norms.


## Practical application

The useful move is not "Country X scores 68". It is anticipating specific
interaction failures.

* **High vs. low power distance** in one team: a manager from a low-power-
  distance culture says "challenge me, I want debate" and reads silence as
  disengagement. Team members from high-power-distance cultures read the
  invitation as a test. Remedy: structured input — written first, round-robin,
  or anonymous — rather than open floor.

* **High vs. low uncertainty avoidance**: one group wants a detailed
  specification before starting; the other wants to prototype and learn. Both
  are competent. Remedy: agree explicitly what is fixed and what is open, in
  writing, rather than each side assuming the other is careless or slow.

* **Individualist vs. collectivist**: individual performance rankings and public
  individual praise land very differently. Remedy: check whether the recognition
  system was designed for one and deployed to the other.


## The serious criticisms

State these whenever you use the model.

* **Ecological fallacy**: scores are national averages. Within-country variance
  exceeds between-country variance on most dimensions. Applying a national score
  to the person in front of you is straightforwardly invalid, and is stereotyping
  with a citation attached.
* **Method**: single company (IBM), single era, mostly male professional
  respondents, dimensions derived by factor analysis on limited items.
* **Nation as unit**: nations are not cultures. India, Nigeria, and Belgium each
  contain multiple.
* **Static**: scores are treated as stable, but cultures change.
* **Alternatives**: GLOBE study, Trompenaars, Schwartz value survey. All
  contested; GLOBE distinguishes practices from values, which is a real
  improvement.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: international recruitment is standard in many health systems.
  Power distance differences directly affect willingness to challenge a senior
  clinician — a patient safety issue, not a diversity nicety. The remedy is
  structural: graded assertiveness protocols and read-backs, which work
  regardless of cultural background. See
  [crew-resource-management](../crew-resource-management/).

* **Software**: distributed teams across time zones and cultures need explicit
  norms about who speaks, how disagreement is registered, and whether silence
  means consent. Write the norms down; do not assume shared defaults.

* **Executive**: in cross-border integration, expect the acquirer's management
  practices to be experienced very differently than intended. Use Hofstede to
  generate questions to ask locally, never as a substitute for asking.
