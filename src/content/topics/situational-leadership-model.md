# Situational leadership model

Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard's situational leadership proposes that there is no
single best leadership style; the effective style depends on the **development
level of the follower for a specific task**.

Two behavioral dimensions:

* **Directive behavior**: telling, structuring, supervising, defining.
* **Supportive behavior**: listening, encouraging, facilitating, involving.

**Evidence: Weak.** Several empirical tests have failed to confirm the
style-to-development-level matching prescriptions, the underlying constructs
are loosely defined and inconsistently measured, and the model has been
revised repeatedly without new evidence. Its real value is as a conversation
structure that gives a manager and a report shared language for asking for
more support or less supervision — a genuine service that does not require the
four-quadrant theory to be true. Better-evidenced neighbors are goal setting
theory and leader-member exchange.

Use when: coaching managers who apply one style to everyone; when a capable
person is being micromanaged, or a struggling person is being abandoned in the
name of autonomy.

Do not use when: you need an evidence-based leadership theory. The model is
popular and weakly supported.


## The four styles

| Style | Directive | Supportive | Name | For a follower who is… |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S1 | High | Low | Directing / Telling | D1: low competence, high commitment — the enthusiastic beginner |
| S2 | High | High | Coaching / Selling | D2: some competence, low commitment — the disillusioned learner |
| S3 | Low | High | Supporting / Participating | D3: high competence, variable commitment — the capable but cautious performer |
| S4 | Low | Low | Delegating | D4: high competence, high commitment — the self-reliant achiever |


## The two classic errors

* **Over-delegating to D1 and D2.** A new person given autonomy and no structure
  in the name of empowerment. They flounder, and it is read as a hiring mistake.
  The D2 stage — competence rising, confidence falling as the person discovers
  how hard the job is — is where most new joiners quit, and it is precisely
  where S2 coaching is needed and is least often given.

* **Over-directing D3 and D4.** An expert supervised as though new. This is the
  fastest way to lose senior people, and the manager usually believes they are
  being helpful.

The critical nuance: **development level is per task, not per person.** A senior
clinician is D4 in clinic and D1 on a new digital system. A staff engineer is D4
in their domain and D1 leading their first team. Treating the person rather than
the task is the model's most common misuse — and it is a misuse of the model, not
a limitation of it.


## Using it well

1. Name the specific task.
2. Assess competence and commitment for that task, with the person, not about
   them.
3. Agree the style together and say it aloud: "for this, I'm going to be quite
   directive for the first month; tell me when that stops being useful."
4. Revisit as competence grows. The failure to re-contract is why S1 becomes
   micromanagement.


## The evidence problem

* Little empirical support for the specific style-to-development matching
  prescriptions; several tests have failed to confirm them.
* The underlying constructs are loosely defined and inconsistently measured.
* The model has been revised repeatedly without new evidence.

Evidence: weak. Its real value is as a **conversation structure** that gives a
manager and a report language for "I need more support here" and "I need you to
back off there" — a genuine service, independent of whether the four-quadrant
theory is true. See [evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).

Better-evidenced neighbors: goal setting theory (specific, difficult goals with
feedback), [self-determination-theory](../self-determination-theory/) for the
autonomy-support mechanism, and leader-member exchange research.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: clinical supervision is formally graded by competence, which
  is situational leadership institutionalized. The gap is on non-clinical tasks
  — a consultant asked to lead a service change is often D1 and treated as D4.

* **Software**: a promoted tech lead is D4 as an engineer and D1 as a manager,
  and typically receives S4 delegation because they are senior. This is the
  standard mechanism by which good engineers become unsupported managers.

* **Executive**: newly appointed executives are D1 on the political system of
  the organization, whatever their track record. The first ninety days need S2,
  and executives are rarely offered it because asking for it looks like weakness.
