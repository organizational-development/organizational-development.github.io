# Discredited instruments

A catalog of popular organizational tools that the evidence does not support,
collected in one place so that they can be recognized and declined.

These are not merely weakly supported, which describes much of this collection
and is not disqualifying. These are instruments where the central claim has been
**tested and not found**, and where using them for a consequential decision —
hiring, promotion, team assignment, or training design — misallocates money and
can be unfair to individuals.

**Evidence: Very weak to refuted.** Each entry below states its own status. The
common pattern: an intuitively appealing typology, an appealing self-recognition
experience, commercial incentives to sustain it, and either no predictive
validity or a directly failed test of the mechanism it claims.

Use when: someone proposes one of these, and you need the specific objection
rather than a general sense of unease.

Do not use when: as a licence to dismiss all assessment. Several instruments in
this repository are well validated — see
[psychological-safety](../psychological-safety/),
[westrum-organizational-culture-typology](../westrum-organizational-culture-typology/),
and [job-characteristics-model](../job-characteristics-model/). Scepticism is a
tool, not a personality.


## Learning styles

**Claim.** People learn better when instruction matches their preferred style —
visual, auditory, kinaesthetic, or one of some seventy other published schemes.

**Status: refuted.** The claim requires a *meshing* interaction: people labelled
visual should outperform when taught visually, and underperform when taught
auditorily. Studies designed to test that interaction — rather than merely
asking people their preference — consistently fail to find it. Reviews have
repeatedly concluded there is no adequate evidence base for using learning
styles in instruction.

**What is true underneath.** People do have preferences, and preferences are
real. Content also has an inherent best modality: teach geography with maps and
music with sound, regardless of who is in the room. Prior knowledge predicts
learning far better than style ever did.

**What to do instead.** Design for the material. Use spacing, retrieval
practice, interleaving, and worked examples — all well supported.


## Myers-Briggs Type Indicator

**Claim.** People fall into sixteen types, and the type predicts suitability for
roles, teams, and communication approaches.

**Status: weak, and inappropriate for consequential decisions.** Three specific
problems. Test–retest reliability is poor; a substantial proportion of people
get a different type on retaking within weeks. The underlying traits are
continuously distributed, so dichotomizing at the mean assigns two nearly
identical people to opposite types. And there is little evidence of predictive
validity for job performance.

**What is true underneath.** Some of the dimensions correlate with better-
supported personality traits, particularly extraversion. The Big Five model has
far better psychometric properties.

**What to do instead.** If you need personality assessment for selection, use a
validated Big Five instrument, and know that structured interviews and work
sample tests predict job performance better than personality does. If you want a
team conversation about difference, say that is what it is, do not use the
result for any decision, and pick something cheaper. See
<https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/big-five-personality-traits>.


## DISC and colour-based type systems

**Claim.** Four types — variously dominance/influence/steadiness/conscientious-
ness, or red/blue/green/yellow — describe behavioral style and predict fit.

**Status: weak.** The same dichotomization problem as MBTI, generally with less
published psychometric work behind it. Commercial variants proliferate without
independent validation. Predictive validity for job performance is not
established.

**What to do instead.** As above. If the goal is a shared vocabulary for
discussing working preferences, any framework will do that, and none of them
should touch a hiring decision.


## Generational categories

**Claim.** Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z have
distinct workplace values requiring different management.

**Status: weak.** Studies attempting to isolate generation from age and period
effects find small and inconsistent differences, typically far smaller than
within-generation variation. Much of what is attributed to generation is life
stage — a 25-year-old in any decade wants different things from a 55-year-old —
or the economic conditions of the moment.

**What to do instead.** Ask people what they want. Segment by role, life stage,
and caring responsibility, which are observable and actionable.


## Others, briefly

* **Graphology** — handwriting analysis for selection. No predictive validity;
  still occasionally used in some markets.
* **Neuro-linguistic programming** — the specific claims about eye movements and
  representational systems have not been supported.
* **Left-brain versus right-brain personality** — hemispheric specialization is
  real for some functions; the personality typology built on it is not.
* **Birth order effects on personality** — large modern studies find effects at
  or near zero.


## Why they persist

Understanding the mechanism helps you argue against them without insulting the
person who proposed them, who is usually acting in good faith.

* **The Barnum effect.** Descriptions vague enough to fit anyone produce strong
  recognition. "You are outgoing but value time alone" fits nearly everybody.
* **Non-judgmental framing.** Every type is presented as equally good, so nobody
  is offended and everyone participates. This is genuinely pleasant and is not
  evidence.
* **Commercial reinforcement.** Certification, licensing, and consultancy
  revenue create a supply of confident advocates.
* **Real experience, wrong explanation.** The workshop *is* useful — people talk
  about how they work together, often for the first time. The value comes from
  the conversation, and it gets attributed to the instrument.


## How to decline one

* Ask what decision the result will inform. If the answer is a hiring,
  promotion, or assignment decision, that is the strongest objection and the
  easiest to make.
* Ask what evidence would show it was not working. Instruments in this list
  rarely have an answer.
* Offer the substitute rather than only the refusal: a structured interview, a
  work sample, a validated instrument, or simply a facilitated conversation with
  no typology attached.
* Do not attack the colleague who proposed it. They had a good workshop once.
  That experience was real; the explanation was wrong.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: personality typing is common in team-building days. It
  becomes a patient safety issue only if it is used to justify who may challenge
  whom — "that is just how reds communicate" is a way of excusing behavior that
  a [just-culture](../just-culture/) approach would examine.

* **Software**: learning styles appear in internal training design, where they
  consume budget that spacing and retrieval practice would spend better.
  Personality typing appears in hiring, where structured interviews and work
  samples predict performance better and are more defensible if challenged.

* **Executive**: the most expensive version is a leadership development
  programme built on a typology, priced per participant, with no outcome
  measure. Ask for the theory of change and the evidence before approving it.
  See [theory-of-change](../theory-of-change/) and
  [evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).


## Limitations

* This list is not exhaustive, and new instruments appear continuously.
* "Discredited" applies to the specific claim tested. Some of these tools have
  benign uses as conversation starters, and this file should not be cited to
  suggest otherwise.
* Evidence changes. If a claim here has been supported by newer research, that
  is a defect worth reporting — see
  [../../CONTRIBUTING.md](../../CONTRIBUTING.md).
