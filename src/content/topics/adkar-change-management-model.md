# ADKAR change management model

Jeff Hiatt's ADKAR model (Prosci) is an individual-level change model. Its
premise: organizational change only happens when individuals change, so manage
the change one person at a time, in sequence.

**Evidence: Weak.** Commercially developed, with essentially no independent
validation. The sequential barrier logic is plausible and clinically useful,
and the specific distinction between Knowledge and Ability is well supported
by the training transfer literature, which finds repeatedly that classroom
learning does not survive contact with real conditions without practice and
environmental support. The five-element structure itself is unproven.

Use when: a defined change affects a defined population and you need to find
where individuals are stuck.

Do not use when: the required change is unknown or emergent. ADKAR presumes a
known target behavior.


## The five elements, in order

1. **Awareness** of the need for change. *Do I know why?*
2. **Desire** to participate and support it. *Do I want to?*
3. **Knowledge** of how to change. *Do I know what to do?*
4. **Ability** to implement the required skills and behaviors. *Can I do it in
   real conditions?*
5. **Reinforcement** to sustain the change. *Does anything keep me doing it?*


## The diagnostic power: the barrier point

The sequence matters. Find the first element that is missing — that is the
**barrier point** — and address it. Interventions aimed past the barrier point
are wasted.

| Barrier point | Wrong intervention | Right intervention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Training | Communication of why, with evidence, from a trusted source |
| Desire | More communication | Address what the person loses; involve them; manage resistance individually |
| Knowledge | Exhortation | Training, documentation, job aids |
| Ability | More training | Practice, coaching, time, tooling, removing real obstacles |
| Reinforcement | Declaring success | Metrics, recognition, consequences, removing the old path |

The most common mistake in every sector: training people who lack Desire. They
attend, they pass, and nothing changes — because the barrier was at step 2.

The second most common: assuming Knowledge equals Ability. Knowing the technique
is not being able to do it under pressure at 3 a.m. with an interruption.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: a new documentation standard. Nurses have Awareness and
  Knowledge; they lack Ability, because the workstation is two corridors away
  and the shift has no slack. No amount of training moves this; moving the
  workstation does.

* **Software**: adopting a new testing practice. Engineers often have Knowledge
  and lack Desire, because the previous three mandated practices were abandoned.
  The barrier is credibility, not competence.

* **Executive**: executives are usually strong on Awareness and Desire — they
  chose the change — and weak on Reinforcement, because reinforcement is boring
  and happens after attention has moved to the next initiative.


## Using it operationally

* Assess each element 1–5 per group, or per person for key individuals.
* Score below 3 marks the barrier point; stop there and work it.
* Reassess after each intervention; barrier points move.
* Combine with an organization-level model. ADKAR tells you about people;
  it says nothing about structure, strategy, or systems.


## Limitations

* Practitioner-derived with limited independent validation. See
  [evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).
* Individual focus omits structural causes; a person can have all five elements
  and still fail inside a system that prevents the behavior.
* Presumes the change is correct, and offers no mechanism for the possibility
  that resistance is information.
