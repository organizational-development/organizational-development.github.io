# Leavitt diamond model

Harold Leavitt's 1965 diamond is the ancestor of most later diagnostic models,
and still the most compact. Four interacting variables:

1. **Task**: the work itself, its goals and its complexity.
2. **People**: the actors, their skills, attitudes, and needs.
3. **Structure**: authority, communication paths, workflow.
4. **Technology**: tools, machines, systems, techniques.

The claim: these four are interdependent, so a deliberate change in any one
produces compensating changes in the others — whether or not those changes were
planned.

**Evidence: Weak.** A conceptual checklist from 1965, never validated. Its
claim — that a deliberate change to one variable produces unplanned changes in
the other three — is supported anecdotally and by the sociotechnical
literature, but the model offers no way to predict which changes or how large.
Use it as a five-minute prompt, not as a diagnostic.

Use when: you want a five-minute check on the second-order effects of a planned
change, especially a technology change.

Do not use when: you need depth. Four variables cannot separate culture from
structure, or leadership from management practice.


## The diagnostic use

For any proposed change, ask what it does to the other three points.

* Introducing a tool (Technology) changes who holds information (Structure),
  what skills matter (People), and how the work is done (Task).
* Restructuring (Structure) changes who can do which tasks and which tools
  become necessary.
* Redefining the task changes required skills and preferred structure.

Leavitt's own point was that change agents choose a favorite point of entry —
technologists change technology, HR changes people, executives change structure
— and then are surprised by the effects at the other three.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: adding a digital triage tool (Technology) shifts decision
  authority from experienced nurses to an algorithm (Structure), devalues a
  skill built over years (People), and changes what triage means (Task). All
  four move; only one was planned.

* **Software**: adopting infrastructure as code changes who can deploy
  (Structure) and makes some operations roles obsolete while creating new ones
  (People). See <https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/maturity-models>.

* **Executive**: a shared services consolidation is presented as a Structure
  change and a cost saving. It is also a Task change for every unit that
  previously had local support, and those units will invent shadow capability.


## Limitations

* Too coarse for serious diagnosis; use it as a checklist, not a model.
* No environment term. Later models added it; Leavitt's does not have one.
