# Switch framework

Chip Heath and Dan Heath's *Switch* (2010) organizes behavior change research
into a metaphor from Jonathan Haidt: a **Rider** (rational mind) on an
**Elephant** (emotional mind), traveling a **Path** (the situation).

The Rider is analytical but easily exhausted and prone to over-analysis. The
Elephant is powerful, emotional, and seeks immediate comfort. The Path is the
environment, which shapes both.

**Evidence: Mixed.** A popular synthesis rather than original research, and
the quality of the underlying work varies by tactic. Well supported: shaping
the environment, defaults and choice architecture, action triggers or
implementation intentions, and social proof. Weakly supported: the Rider and
Elephant dichotomy, which is a simplification of dual-process theory that the
theory itself no longer cleanly supports. Bright spots derives from
solution-focused practice and has modest evidence.

Use when: designing a change that requires many individuals to behave
differently, and previous attempts at persuasion have failed.

Do not use when: the change is structural or strategic rather than behavioral.


## The three parts and nine tactics

**Direct the Rider** — what looks like resistance is often lack of clarity.

1. **Follow the bright spots.** Find where it already works here, and clone it.
   Studying successes is more actionable than studying failures.
2. **Script the critical moves.** Not "be more patient-centered" but "sit down
   when speaking to a patient". Ambiguity is the enemy of execution.
3. **Point to the destination.** A vivid, concrete picture of the outcome, and
   why it is worth it. Heaths call it a "destination postcard".

**Motivate the Elephant** — what looks like laziness is often exhaustion.

4. **Find the feeling.** Knowing is not enough; make people feel something. One
   patient's story beats a dataset for motivation, though not for diagnosis.
5. **Shrink the change.** Reduce the first step until it is unmissably small.
   Progress creates motivation more reliably than motivation creates progress.
6. **Grow your people.** Cultivate identity ("we are the kind of team that…")
   and a growth mindset. Identity-based change outlasts incentive-based change.

**Shape the Path** — what looks like a people problem is often a situation
problem.

7. **Tweak the environment.** Change the situation and behavior follows.
   The highest-leverage and most-neglected tactic.
8. **Build habits.** Use action triggers: "when X happens, I will do Y."
   Checklists and defaults are habits made external.
9. **Rally the herd.** Behavior is contagious. Publicize what others are already
   doing, especially peers.


## The core claim

"What looks like a people problem is often a situation problem." Before
attributing failure to attitude, examine the Path. This is the fundamental
attribution error stated as management advice, and it is the most valuable
sentence in the book.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: bright spots — find the ward with the lowest infection rate
  and study it, rather than benchmarking against the national mean. Path —
  default order sets and pre-filled checklists change prescribing behavior more
  than education campaigns do.

* **Software**: scripting critical moves means a concrete definition of done,
  not "improve quality". Shaping the path means making the safe action the
  default: pre-commit hooks, templates, paved roads. If the right way is harder
  than the wrong way, exhortation will not close the gap.

* **Executive**: shrink the change — a 90-day pilot on one business unit gets
  agreement that a three-year transformation cannot. Find the feeling — take the
  executive team to observe the actual work, in person, once.


## Limitations

* Popular synthesis rather than original research; the underlying studies vary
  in quality.
* The Rider/Elephant dichotomy is a simplification of dual-process theory.
* Individual-behavior focus; weak on power, structure, and politics.
* Risk of manipulation: "find the feeling" is a persuasion technique, and
  deserves the ethical scrutiny in [od-ethics](../od-ethics/).
