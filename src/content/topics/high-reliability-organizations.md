# High reliability organizations

High reliability organization (HRO) theory studies organizations that operate in
hazardous, complex conditions and yet have very few catastrophic failures:
aircraft carriers, air traffic control, nuclear power, wildland firefighting.
The central work is Karl Weick and Kathleen Sutcliffe's *Managing the
Unexpected*.

Their finding: reliability does not come from eliminating error. It comes from
**noticing small anomalies early and responding to them before they compound**.
HROs are not error-free; they are error-catching.

**Evidence: Moderate.** A rich and credible case study base from aircraft
carriers, air traffic control, and nuclear operations, and the mindful
organizing construct has been operationalized into validated survey scales
with associations to safety outcomes in health care. Causal outcome evidence
remains limited, and the founding organizations are unusual in resources and
staffing, which complicates transfer. Normalization of deviance is well
documented across multiple major accident investigations.

Use when: the work has severe consequences of failure; when incidents recur
despite procedural compliance; when an organization is "safe on paper".

Do not use when: consequences of failure are low. HRO practices are expensive
in attention, and attention is finite.


## The five principles of mindful organizing

**Anticipation — three principles for noticing early**

1. **Preoccupation with failure.** Treat any lapse as a symptom of something
   systemic. Actively seek near misses and reward reporting. Success is
   interpreted as a reason for vigilance, not for confidence — because a
   long run without failure is exactly when drift accumulates unnoticed.

2. **Reluctance to simplify.** Resist tidy explanations. "Human error" and
   "communication failure" are labels that end inquiry. HROs deliberately
   maintain diverse perspectives and encourage skepticism about the accepted
   account.

3. **Sensitivity to operations.** Continuous attention to the front line and to
   what is actually happening now, as opposed to what the plan says. Leaders
   spend time where the work is done, and the situational picture is updated
   constantly rather than at reporting intervals.

**Containment — two principles for coping once something goes wrong**

4. **Commitment to resilience.** Assume failures will occur; build the capacity
   to detect, contain, and recover. Practice improvisation. Maintain slack and
   redundancy — the first things cut by efficiency programs.

5. **Deference to expertise.** During a problem, decisions migrate to the person
   with the most relevant expertise, regardless of rank, and migrate back
   afterwards. Formal authority does not track situational knowledge.


## Normalization of deviance

Diane Vaughan's concept from her study of the Challenger disaster: a small
deviation from standard occurs, nothing bad happens, and the deviation becomes
the new normal. Repeated, the accepted standard drifts far from the designed
one, and each individual step was locally reasonable.

The countermeasure is not more rules. It is:

* Noticing and naming drift explicitly, at the moment it occurs.
* Asking why the deviation is attractive — it is usually faster, and the
  official procedure is usually badly designed for real conditions.
* Fixing the procedure rather than exhorting compliance with an unworkable one.
  Persistent workarounds are design feedback.


## Practices that operationalize the principles

* Near-miss reporting with visible, thanked reporters. See
  [just-culture](../just-culture/).
* Structured briefings and debriefings around every significant operation. See
  [after-action-review](../after-action-review/),
  [crew-resource-management](../crew-resource-management/).
* Daily short safety huddles that surface anticipated risks for the coming
  shift or day — cheap, and among the highest-yield HRO practices available.
* Explicit stop authority, held by anyone, protected in policy and in practice.
* Rotating an outside perspective through reviews to counter simplification.
* Protected slack and cross-skilling for resilience.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: the primary adopter outside the military. Daily safety
  huddles, escalation protocols, stop-the-line authority for any staff member,
  and structured handover are all HRO practices. The frequent implementation
  gap is deference to expertise: the principle is adopted in policy while a
  junior who stops a procedure still faces career consequences. The policy is
  not the practice.

* **Software**: site reliability engineering is HRO applied. Error budgets are
  preoccupation with failure quantified; incident command is deference to
  expertise formalized; game days and chaos engineering are commitment to
  resilience practiced. Normalization of deviance shows up as the alert everyone
  ignores and the runbook step everyone skips.

* **Executive**: the executive-level HRO question is whether small anomalies
  reach you at all, and how they arrive. If your information is monthly,
  aggregated, and filtered through people whose performance it reflects, you
  have anticipation capability approaching zero. Also examine which efficiency
  programs removed the slack that resilience requires.


## Limitations

* Derived from a small number of unusual organizations with unusual resources.
* Expensive in attention, staffing, and slack; hard to sustain under cost
  pressure.
* "HRO" is often adopted as branding — a policy and a poster — without the
  underlying practices, which is worse than not adopting it, because it creates
  the belief that the problem is handled.
