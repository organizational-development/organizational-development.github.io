# Process consultation

Edgar Schein's process consultation is a stance rather than a technique. Its
definition: "the creation of a relationship with the client that permits the
client to perceive, understand, and act on the process events that occur in the
client's internal and external environment in order to improve the situation as
defined by the client."

The consultant's expertise is in **process** — how work is done, how people
interact, how decisions are made — not in content. The client keeps ownership of
the problem.

**Evidence: Weak as research, strong as practitioner consensus.** No controlled
evidence that the process consultation stance outperforms expert or
doctor-patient consulting. It is endorsed near-universally in the OD literature,
and its central claim — that solutions the client owns survive longer than
solutions delivered to them — is consistent with the participation and
internalized motivation literatures. Treat it as a well-argued professional
stance rather than a demonstrated method.

Use when: the client's diagnosis may be wrong; when the solution must be owned
locally; when previous expert recommendations were accepted and not implemented.

Do not use when: the client genuinely needs specialist content expertise and
knows it — a legal opinion, a security assessment, a clinical protocol.


## Schein's three models of helping

1. **Expert / purchase of expertise.** The client defines the problem and buys
   an answer. Works only if the client diagnosed correctly, communicated
   accurately, and the expert's answer is implementable in their context. Three
   fragile assumptions.

2. **Doctor-patient.** The client presents symptoms; the consultant diagnoses
   and prescribes. Fails when the client will not reveal the real symptoms, will
   not accept the diagnosis, or will not take the medicine. The most seductive
   model for consultants and the most commonly resisted by clients.

3. **Process consultation.** The consultant helps the client diagnose and solve
   their own problem. Slower to satisfy, more durable in effect, and the only
   model that builds client capability.

All three are legitimate. The error is defaulting to model 1 or 2 when the
situation calls for 3 — which happens because 1 and 2 feel like value delivery
and 3 feels like doing nothing.


## Principles of humble inquiry

Schein's ten principles, condensed to the ones that carry the weight:

* **Always try to be helpful.** Helping is the purpose, not being right.
* **Always stay in touch with the current reality.** Yours and theirs.
* **Access your ignorance.** The most useful question is the one you genuinely
  do not know the answer to. Questions you know the answer to are advocacy in
  disguise, and clients can tell.
* **Everything you do is an intervention.** There is no neutral observation. The
  first question you ask changes the system.
* **It is the client who owns the problem and the solution.** You will leave.
* **Go with the flow.** Work with the client's energy and readiness, not your
  plan.
* **Timing is crucial.** The same intervention lands or fails depending entirely
  on when it is offered.
* **When in doubt, share the problem.** Admitting uncertainty to the client is a
  legitimate move, and it models the behavior you want.


## Humble inquiry in practice

Four kinds of question, in descending order of usefulness for this stance:

* **Humble inquiry**: minimally prompting, maximum curiosity. "Tell me more."
  "What happened next?"
* **Diagnostic inquiry**: steering attention. "How did you feel about that?"
  "What do you think caused it?"
* **Confrontational inquiry**: inserting your own idea as a question. "Could it
  be that the deadline is the problem?" Useful, but it is your content now.
* **Process-oriented inquiry**: turning attention to the interaction itself.
  "What's happening between us right now?" The most powerful and the most easily
  mistimed.


## Group process observation

What a process consultant watches while a group works:

* Who speaks, for how long, and after whom.
* Who is interrupted, and by whom.
* How decisions actually get made: consensus, default, silence, or the loudest.
* What topics produce a change in energy, humor, or pace.
* What is raised and dropped without response.
* What is discussed in the corridor and not in the room.

Feeding these observations back — descriptively, without interpretation — is
often the entire intervention.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: a team asks for "communication training". Process
  consultation observes three handovers first, and reports what it saw: the
  handover is conducted standing in a corridor, interrupted four times, with no
  structured format and no read-back. That description does more than a course.

* **Software**: a team asks for an architecture review. The process consultant
  notices that three architecture decisions have been made and reversed, always
  after the same person's objection, always outside the meeting. The content
  question is not the problem.

* **Executive**: an executive team asks for a strategy facilitator. The
  observable process finding — that the CEO speaks first on every item and the
  discussion converges within ninety seconds — is more valuable than any
  strategy framework, and much harder to deliver.


## Limitations

* Slow, and easily read as passive or evasive by clients expecting answers.
* Requires the practitioner to tolerate not being the expert, which is
  professionally uncomfortable.
* Contract for it explicitly at entry, or you will be pulled into the doctor
  role by week three. See [entry-and-contracting](../entry-and-contracting/).
