# Maslow hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow's hierarchy (1943) proposes that human needs are ordered, and
that lower needs must be substantially met before higher ones motivate behavior.

1. **Physiological** — food, water, warmth, rest.
2. **Safety** — security, stability, freedom from fear.
3. **Belonging and love** — relationships, acceptance, connection.
4. **Esteem** — recognition, status, respect, achievement.
5. **Self-actualization** — realizing one's potential; becoming what one can be.

Maslow later added cognitive, aesthetic, and self-transcendence needs. The
famous pyramid diagram does not appear in his work; it was added by later
textbook authors.

**Evidence: Weak.** The need categories have reasonable support; the strict
ordering does not. People pursue esteem and self-actualization while lower needs
are unmet, routinely and across cultures, and cross-cultural work finds the
ordering and relative importance vary. The familiar pyramid does not appear in
Maslow's own writing. Alderfer's ERG formulation, with no strict prepotency and
an explicit frustration-regression mechanism, fits the data better. One
practical implication survives: active safety needs block higher-order
interventions.

Use when: a shared vocabulary is needed with a general audience, or when
explaining why higher-order interventions fail in an organization where people
fear for their jobs.

Do not use when: predicting behavior or designing an intervention. The strict
hierarchy is not empirically supported.


## The evidence

* The **existence** of the need categories has reasonable support.
* The **strict ordering** does not. People pursue esteem and self-actualization
  while lower needs are unmet — routinely and across cultures.
* Cross-cultural work finds need categories are broadly universal while their
  ordering and relative importance are not.
* Clayton Alderfer's **ERG theory** (Existence, Relatedness, Growth) is a better
  formulation: three categories, no strict prepotency, and an explicit
  *frustration-regression* mechanism — when growth is blocked, people intensify
  demands on relatedness or existence. That mechanism explains a common
  workplace pattern: when development is frozen, pay and conditions disputes
  rise.

Evidence: weak for the hierarchy, moderate for the categories. See
[evidence-based-practice](../evidence-based-practice/).


## The one durable practical implication

**You cannot run higher-order interventions on people whose safety needs are
active.** Restructuring announcements, redundancy consultations, contract
uncertainty, or unsafe staffing all activate level 2. During that period:

* Vision workshops, values exercises, and engagement programs will be received
  as insulting.
* Survey scores will fall regardless of intervention quality.
* The right intervention is information: what is happening, when, and what it
  means for me. Certainty beats reassurance; "we don't know yet, and we'll tell
  you on the 14th" outperforms optimistic vagueness.

This is the reason a culture program launched during a redundancy round fails,
and the reason its failure is misattributed to the program design.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: staff who cannot reliably take a break, get a meal, or feel
  physically safe on shift are at levels 1 and 2. Wellbeing programs pitched at
  levels 4 and 5 in that context provoke anger, correctly.

* **Software**: after a layoff announcement, engineering teams are at level 2
  for months. Expect risk aversion, reduced discretionary effort, and quiet job
  searching. Ambitious change initiatives launched in that window fail and are
  then remembered as bad ideas.

* **Executive**: executives are themselves subject to this. An executive whose
  own position is uncertain during a merger will behave defensively, and the
  organization will read it. Name it, at least to yourself.


## Limitations

* The hierarchy is over-taught and over-trusted relative to its evidence.
* It has been used to justify paternalism — deciding for people which needs they
  should have met.
* Prefer [self-determination-theory](../self-determination-theory/) for anything
  consequential.
