# Competing values framework

Robert Quinn and Kim Cameron's competing values framework (CVF) maps culture on
two axes and produces four culture types. Its associated instrument, the
Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI), is among the most widely
used culture measures.

Axes:

* **Flexibility and discretion** ↔ **Stability and control**
* **Internal focus and integration** ↔ **External focus and differentiation**

**Evidence: Moderate to good.** Among the better-evidenced culture instruments.
The OCAI has a substantial psychometric literature and the two-dimensional
structure has been replicated across many samples. Predictive validity is mixed:
culture type correlates with some effectiveness measures, but the framework
makes no causal claim that a given profile produces performance in a given
context, and it should not be read as though it does. It measures orientation,
not Schein's third level.

Use when: you need a comparable, quantified read on culture, especially to
compare current vs. preferred, or unit vs. unit, or acquirer vs. acquired.

Do not use when: you need the deep assumptions. CVF measures orientation, not
Schein's third level. See
[schein-model-of-organizational-culture](../schein-model-of-organizational-culture/).


## The four types

| Type | Axes | Orientation | Leader role | Value drivers | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clan** | Flexible + internal | Collaborate. Family-like, mentoring, participation, loyalty | Facilitator, mentor | Commitment, development, communication | Cosiness; conflict avoidance; underperformance tolerated |
| **Adhocracy** | Flexible + external | Create. Entrepreneurial, risk-taking, innovative | Innovator, visionary | Innovative output, agility | Chaos; no follow-through; reinvention |
| **Market** | Controlled + external | Compete. Results-oriented, goal-driven, demanding | Hard driver, competitor | Market share, profitability, goal achievement | Burnout; internal rivalry; short-termism |
| **Hierarchy** | Controlled + internal | Control. Structured, procedural, efficiency-focused | Coordinator, monitor | Efficiency, timeliness, consistency | Bureaucracy; slow adaptation; process over purpose |


## The "competing" part

The framework's name is the point: these values genuinely conflict. Flexibility
trades against control; internal cohesion trades against external
responsiveness. An organization cannot maximize all four. Leadership work is
choosing the profile the strategy requires and accepting what is given up.

Most executive teams, asked what culture they want, describe all four. That
answer is the finding.


## The OCAI method

Respondents divide 100 points across four descriptions, across six dimensions:
dominant characteristics, leadership, management of employees, organizational
glue, strategic emphasis, criteria for success. Done twice: **now** and
**preferred in five years**.

The gap between now and preferred, plotted as two overlaid quadrilaterals, is
the conversation. Useful readings:

* Large gap on one type: a clear directional mandate.
* Leadership and frontline producing different "now" profiles: leadership does
  not know its own organization.
* Different units with incompatible profiles: expect friction at their boundary,
  and do not try to make them identical.
* Everyone wants more Clan: usually a signal of exhaustion, not a culture
  strategy.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: typically Hierarchy-dominant, for defensible reasons —
  safety, regulation, standardization. The tension is that innovation and
  responsiveness require Adhocracy, and staff wellbeing pulls toward Clan.
  Ambitions to become "more innovative" without relaxing any control mechanism
  are incoherent, and staff know it.

* **Software**: startups begin Adhocracy and drift toward Market as they scale,
  then add Hierarchy as they mature. Founders often experience this necessary
  drift as decline. Naming the intended profile at each stage defuses a lot of
  unproductive nostalgia.

* **Executive**: after an acquisition, run OCAI on both organizations before
  integration planning. An Adhocracy acquired by a Hierarchy will lose the
  people who created the value being purchased, unless the integration
  explicitly protects a different profile.


## Limitations

* Four boxes flatten real complexity; most units are mixtures.
* Self-report, and vulnerable to what respondents think is the desired answer.
* Measures perceptions and orientation, not the underlying assumptions that
  drive behavior.
* No causal claim: the framework does not establish that a profile *causes*
  performance in a given context.
