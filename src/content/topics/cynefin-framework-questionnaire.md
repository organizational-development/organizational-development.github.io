# Cynefin Framework Questionnaire

To use Cynefin as a diagnostic, use this questionnaire. Unlike the others here,
it does not score a single scale: it asks a series of questions whose answers
place a **specific situation** in a domain, and then prescribes the response
appropriate to that domain.

Run it on one named situation at a time. A department is not in a domain; a
particular problem is.

See [cynefin-framework](../cynefin-framework/).

**Evidence: Weak empirically, high conceptual utility.** Domain assignment is a
judgment and two competent people can disagree. The disagreement is usually the
productive part.

## Step 1: Domain placement

Score each statement 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

**Clear indicators**

2. There is an established best practice, and everyone would name the same one.
   `[ ]`
3. If we simply categorized the situation correctly, the right action would
   follow automatically. `[ ]`

**Complicated indicators**

4. There is a right answer, and an expert could find it with enough analysis and
   data. `[ ]`
5. Several defensible approaches exist, and choosing between them requires
   expertise rather than experimentation. `[ ]`
6. We could write a specification for the solution before building it. `[ ]`

**Complex indicators**

7. The situation responds to our interventions, so what worked last time may not
   work now. `[ ]`
8. We can explain why things happened afterwards, but could not have predicted
   them beforehand. `[ ]`
9. Competent, well-informed people genuinely disagree about what will happen if
   we act. `[ ]`
10. Any business case we write will be a guess dressed as a forecast. `[ ]`

**Chaotic indicators**

11. The situation is actively deteriorating and no stable pattern is
    discernible. `[ ]`
12. Doing nothing while we analyze is the worst available option. `[ ]`

**Confused indicators**

13. Different people in the room are confidently applying incompatible
    methods. `[ ]`
14. We are arguing about *how* to proceed more than about *what* to do. `[ ]`


## Step 2: Read the placement

* Highest scores on 1–3 → **Clear**. Sense, categorize, respond. Apply best
  practice. Watch for complacency, and for the cliff into Chaotic when
  conditions change and nobody notices.

* Highest scores on 4–6 → **Complicated**. Sense, analyze, respond. Use experts,
  apply good practice, accept there are several valid answers. Watch for expert
  overconfidence and entrained thinking.

* Highest scores on 7–10 → **Complex**. Probe, sense, respond. Run parallel
  safe-to-fail experiments; amplify what works, dampen what does not. Watch for
  the demand for a guaranteed outcome, which forces the problem to be treated as
  Complicated.

* Highest scores on 11–12 → **Chaotic**. Act, sense, respond. Stabilize first,
  then move the situation into Complex. Watch for anyone enjoying the authority
  that crisis grants.

* High scores on 13–14 regardless of the rest → **Confused**. Break the
  situation into parts and place each part separately. This is the most common
  real state, and naming it is the intervention.


## Step 3: The mismatch check

The most valuable question in the set.

- Question: Which domain does our current *management approach* assume?
  (Detailed multi-year plan and business case = Complicated. Best-practice
  rollout = Clear. Portfolio of experiments = Complex. Command and stabilize =
  Chaotic.)

- Question: Does that match the domain we placed the situation in?
  - Rating (1–5, where 5 = matches well): [ ]

A low score here is the finding. **The characteristic organizational error is
managing a Complex problem as though it were Complicated**: a detailed plan, a
business case promising a specific number, and a consultant selling a known
answer. Culture change, leadership behavior, and most organizational change sit
in Complex and are almost always managed as Complicated.

## Step 5: Analysis

* **Domains are situational and they move.** Re-place the situation when
  conditions change. A stable Clear process becomes Chaotic the moment its
  assumptions break, and that transition is the dangerous one because nobody is
  watching for it.

* **Mixed programs need mixed management.** A merger's legal and financial
  integration is Complicated; its cultural integration is Complex. Running both
  under one plan with one set of milestones guarantees the cultural half is
  judged by the wrong criteria and declared complete when it is not.

* **Resist the demand to convert Complex into Complicated for governance
  convenience.** The demand is understandable — boards want forecasts — and
  meeting it produces a plan that cannot adapt. The honest response is a
  portfolio with a stated learning cadence and a defined spend, not a fabricated
  forecast.

* **Do not use this as a permanent classification.** Cynefin is a sense-making
  aid for a conversation, not a taxonomy for a filing system.
