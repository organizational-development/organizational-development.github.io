# Force field analysis

Kurt Lewin's force field analysis models any current state as a **quasi-
stationary equilibrium**: a balance between driving forces pushing toward change
and restraining forces holding the status quo. Nothing is static; the apparent
stability is a stalemate.

**Evidence: Weak as a measurement tool, moderate as a principle.** The ratings
are subjective and the arithmetic is a conversation device. The underlying
principle is better supported: behavioral research on friction, defaults, and
choice architecture consistently finds that removing barriers changes behavior
more reliably and more cheaply than increasing persuasion or incentive. Trust
the principle; do not trust the numbers.

Use when: you need to understand why a change has stalled, or to plan a change
before launching it.

Do not use when: nothing is holding the status quo in place, which is rare
enough to be worth doubting.


## The central insight

Most change efforts add driving forces: more urgency, more communication, more
incentives, more executive pressure. Lewin's finding is that **removing
restraining forces is usually more effective**, because adding driving force
raises tension in the system and provokes counter-pressure.

Push harder and you get compliance plus resentment. Remove the obstacle and you
get movement at lower cost.


## How to run it

1. State the change clearly, as a target state, not a slogan.
2. List driving forces. Rate each 1–5 for strength.
3. List restraining forces. Rate each 1–5.
4. Draw them as opposing arrows, sized by rating.
5. For each restraining force, ask: can we remove it, weaken it, or convert it?
6. Only then consider adding driving force.

Do this with the people who will be restrained, not about them. The restraining
forces list is nearly always wrong when written by leadership alone.


## Typical restraining forces

* Loss of status, autonomy, or identity.
* A reward system that pays for the old behavior.
* Genuine capability gaps that nobody wants to admit.
* Prior failed changes: "we tried this in 2019".
* Physical or technical constraints: rooms, rotas, systems, licences.
* Workload — no slack in which to change anything.

The last one is the most common and the least often addressed. People are not
resisting; they have no capacity.


## Examples by audience

* **Health care**: hand hygiene compliance. Driving forces: audits, posters,
  professional duty. Restraining forces: dispensers positioned wrong, gloves
  taking longer, skin damage, understaffed shifts. Moving the dispenser beats
  another poster.

* **Software**: adopting trunk-based development. Driving: faster delivery.
  Restraining: a two-hour test suite, a manual approval board, and one person
  who owns release and likes owning it. Address the test suite first.

* **Executive**: a return-to-office mandate. Driving forces are asserted
  (collaboration, culture). Restraining forces are concrete (commute cost,
  caregiving, desk shortage, teams already hired remote). Mandates that ignore
  concrete restraints produce attrition rather than presence.


## Limitations

* Subjective ratings; the numbers are a conversation device, not measurement.
* Static snapshot — forces change as the change proceeds.
* Can produce a comforting list with no owner attached. Assign each restraining
  force a named owner and a date, or the analysis is decorative.
