## Introduction

Organizational development is a planned, systematic, evidence-informed approach
to improving an organization's capability, effectiveness, and long-term health,
through the alignment of its strategy, structure, culture, and people.

It draws on psychology, sociology, systems theory, and improvement science. Its
distinguishing commitment is that organizations are human systems: they are
changed by working with the people in them, not by issuing instructions to them.


### Who this is for

This guide is written for knowledge workers in three settings.

* **Health care**: doctors, nurses, allied health professionals, administrators,
  clinical directors, improvement leads.
* **Software**: designers, developers, testers, architects, engineering
  managers, product leaders.
* **Senior executives**: CEO, CIO, CTO, CHRO, and their equivalents in public
  and non-profit organizations.

These settings look different and share more than they differ. All three are
characterized by:

* **High autonomy.** The work cannot be fully specified in advance, and the
  person doing it knows more about it than their manager does.
* **High interdependence.** Individual excellence is insufficient; outcomes are
  produced by handoffs, coordination, and shared systems.
* **High consequence of error**, whether measured in harm, in money, or in
  reputation.
* **Professional identity that outranks organizational identity.** A physician
  is a physician before they are an employee of this hospital. A staff engineer
  is an engineer before they are an employee of this firm. This is the single
  most important fact for anyone attempting change in these settings: change
  that threatens professional judgment will be resisted by competent, committed,
  well-intentioned people, and no amount of communication will resolve it.

Wherever a framework is explained below, examples show how it lands in each of
the three settings.

### The ten things that matter most

If you read nothing else:

1. **Diagnose before you intervene.** The most expensive error in the field is
   selecting an intervention — a reorganization, an offsite, a values launch, a
   tool rollout — before establishing what is wrong.

2. **Distinguish transformational from transactional problems.** A structural
   fix will not solve a cultural problem, and a culture workshop will not solve
   a queueing problem. See [Burke-Litwin](#burke-litwin-causal-model).

3. **What looks like a people problem is usually a situation problem.** Before
   attributing failure to attitude or capability, examine the workload, the
   incentives, the tooling, and the decision rights.

4. **Remove restraining forces before adding driving force.** Pushing harder
   raises tension and provokes counter-pressure. Removing the obstacle is
   cheaper and more durable. See [force field analysis](#force-field-analysis).

5. **Psychological safety is the highest-leverage team variable**, and it is
   destroyed by one badly handled piece of bad news. See
   [psychological safety](#psychological-safety).

6. **Feed data back to the people it came from, before you report it upward.**
   This is both an ethical commitment and the mechanism by which data produces
   change.

7. **Never run a survey you cannot act on.** An unactioned survey leaves trust
   lower than before you asked.

8. **Expect the performance dip.** Every change that requires new competence
   makes people temporarily worse at their jobs. Programs are abandoned in the
   dip, one week before they would have worked.

9. **Measure with a baseline, a prediction, and a balancing measure**, or you
   are not measuring, you are narrating.

10. **Not all models are equally supported.** Psychological safety, motivation
    theory, and work design have strong evidence. Kotter, ADKAR, Tuckman, and
    Kübler-Ross do not. Weakly supported models can still be good vocabulary;
    they are not good predictions.


## Part 1: Foundations

### What organizational development is

Working definition:

> Organizational development is a planned, system-wide effort, managed from the
> top, to increase organizational effectiveness and health through planned
> interventions in the organization's processes, using behavioral science
> knowledge.

Richard Beckhard's classic definition, still the clearest. Four things follow
from it:

* **Planned.** OD is deliberate, not the accumulation of ad hoc management
  decisions.
* **System-wide.** The unit of analysis is the system, even when the
  intervention is local. An improvement in one unit that exports harm to another
  is not an improvement.
* **Effectiveness and health.** Both. A high-performing organization that burns
  its people is not developed; it is borrowing.
* **Behavioral science knowledge.** OD claims an evidence base. It is obliged to
  behave as if that claim is true. See
  [evidence-based practice](#evidence-based-practice).

The core distinction that runs through the whole field:

* **Organization design** is structural: operating models, reporting lines,
  decision rights, processes, metrics. It answers "how is the work divided and
  coordinated?"
* **Organization development** is behavioral: values, norms, team dynamics, ways
  of working, capability. It answers "how do people here actually behave, and
  what would make them able to do better?"

Both are necessary. Programs that do only design produce new boxes and identical
behavior. Programs that do only development produce goodwill that dissipates
against unchanged structural constraints.


### What organizational development is not

| OD is not | What that is | Where they differ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Change management | Moving a defined population from state A to state B | Change management assumes the destination is known. OD builds the capacity to adapt, including to destinations not yet identified. |
| Organization design | Structure, decision rights, operating model | Design changes the box diagram; development changes what happens inside it. |
| HR | Employment lifecycle: hiring, pay, relations, compliance | OD often sits in HR and is not HR. HR administers the system; OD changes it. |
| Learning and development | Building individual skills | L&D is one OD intervention among many, and is the intervention most often misapplied to non-skill problems. |
| Process improvement | Lean, six sigma, value stream mapping | Technostructural interventions are part of OD's toolkit; OD additionally addresses culture, leadership, and human process. |
| Culture programs | Values statements, engagement campaigns | Values statements are the *weakest* culture lever available. See [how to actually change culture](#how-to-actually-change-culture). |

**The training reflex.** The most common misapplication in all three audiences:
a problem is identified, and the response is training. Training is the correct
response only when the barrier is genuinely a knowledge gap. It is the wrong
response when the barrier is desire, ability under real conditions, incentives,
workload, or structure — which it usually is. See
[ADKAR](#adkar-change-management-model), whose main practical value is
identifying exactly this.


### The values and ethics of OD

OD is unusual in having an explicit value base, and the values create real
conflicts rather than resolving them.

Core values of the tradition:

* **Humanism**: people are ends, not merely means to productivity.
* **Participation**: those affected help shape the change.
* **Transparency**: data is fed back to those it came from.
* **Client self-sufficiency**: the goal is a system that no longer needs you.
* **Whole-system view**: harm exported elsewhere is not improvement.

The standing conflicts, which you should expect rather than be surprised by:

* **Who is the client?** The sponsor pays; the workforce is affected. When
  findings implicate the sponsor, the only thing protecting the work is the
  contract you made at entry.

* **Consent under power asymmetry.** An employee invited to a "voluntary"
  session by their own director has not freely consented. Design real opt-outs
  and never report participation lists.

* **Surveys as extraction.** Asking about problems you have no intent or
  authority to fix is harmful, and reliably lowers trust below the pre-survey
  baseline.

* **Confidentiality vs. duty to disclose.** Patient safety risk, harassment,
  fraud, and regulatory breach cannot be held in confidence. State the
  exceptions before every interview, not after one occurs.

* **Anonymity that is not anonymous.** Demographic cuts on small teams
  re-identify people. Set a minimum reporting threshold — commonly five to ten
  responses — and hold it when an executive pushes.

* **Manufactured consent.** Consultation theater — sessions run after the
  decision is made — does more damage than not consulting, because it teaches
  people that participation is fake.

Data handling: collect the minimum, separate identifiers early, state and honor
retention periods, know your regime (GDPR/UK GDPR, HIPAA, works council and
union agreements), and check processing terms before putting employee free text
into any external tool.

**The practical test.** Before acting, ask: would I be comfortable if every
participant read the full contract, the raw method, and the report to the
sponsor? If not, change something — usually the contract.

See [topics/od-ethics](topics/od-ethics/).


### Evidence-based practice

Evidence-based management means combining four sources of evidence, none
sufficient alone:

1. **Scientific evidence** — research findings, preferably meta-analyses.
2. **Organizational evidence** — your own data: turnover, incidents, cycle time,
   survey trends.
3. **Stakeholder evidence** — the values, concerns, and knowledge of those
   affected.
4. **Practitioner expertise** — your judgment, treated as evidence to check
   rather than as authority.

The six steps: **Ask** an answerable question, **Acquire** evidence, **Appraise**
its quality, **Aggregate** the sources, **Apply**, **Assess** the result.

**Evidence strength across popular models.** Practitioners are rarely told which
of their tools are supported. A rough guide, used throughout this document:

| Model | Evidence | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Psychological safety | Strong | Large literature; robust link to learning and error reporting |
| Self-determination theory | Strong | Decades of experimental support |
| Job characteristics model | Strong | Meta-analytically supported |
| Goal setting theory | Strong | Among the best-supported findings in the field |
| Rogers diffusion attributes | Strong | Hundreds of empirical diffusion studies |
| Westrum typology | Good | Validated and linked to delivery and organizational performance |
| Transformational leadership | Moderate | Real effects, contested measurement |
| Kotter 8-step | Weak | Practitioner-derived; rarely tested as a whole |
| ADKAR | Weak | Useful checklist; little independent validation |
| Tuckman stages | Weak | Memorable; groups do not reliably proceed in order |
| Lencioni five dysfunctions | Weak | A business fable, not research |
| Situational leadership | Weak | Style-matching prescriptions repeatedly unconfirmed |
| Maturity models | Weak | Descriptive; level progression rarely predicts outcomes |
| Herzberg two-factor | Mixed | Two-factor structure likely a method artifact |
| Maslow hierarchy | Weak | Categories plausible; strict ordering unsupported |
| Hofstede dimensions | Contested | Influential; criticized on method and level of analysis |
| Kübler-Ross applied to change | Very weak | Derived from bereavement; never validated for organizations |
| Learning styles | Refuted | Do not build interventions on this |
| MBTI for selection or development | Weak | Low test-retest reliability; avoid for consequential decisions |

Weak evidence does not mean useless. A weakly supported model can be a good
**communication device**: a shared vocabulary that helps a group discuss a real
problem. The error is treating it as a **causal claim** and predicting outcomes
from it.

Practical habits:

* Ask "what would change my mind?" before launching an intervention.
* Distinguish "no evidence of effect" from "evidence of no effect".
* Beware the consultant's citation chain: a claim repeated in ten blog posts
  that cite each other is one claim, not ten.
* Beware benchmark envy: "Google does it" is not evidence it will work at a
  200-bed district hospital.

**Health care:** clinicians already practice evidence-based medicine and will
hold management claims to the same standard. This is an advantage — bring the
effect size. **Software:** engineers respond to instrumented internal evidence
far more than to external frameworks; show the pipeline data. **Executive:** the
highest-value use is challenging an expensive committed decision with "what
evidence would tell us this worked?"

See [topics/evidence-based-practice](topics/evidence-based-practice/).


### Systems thinking

Systems thinking explains recurring behavior by structure rather than by
character. Core concepts:

* **Stocks and flows.** Stocks accumulate — backlog, staff, trust, technical
  debt — and change slowly. Most impatience in change programs is impatience
  with a stock.
* **Reinforcing loops** amplify; **balancing loops** resist and seek a goal.
  Most resistance to change is a balancing loop doing its job, protecting
  something the system values.
* **Delays** cause overshoot, oscillation, and abandonment just before success.
* **Leverage points.** Donella Meadows ranked them: low leverage is parameters
  and targets; high leverage is rules, information flows, goals, and paradigm.
  Most management attention goes to the lowest-leverage end.

**System archetypes** worth recognizing:

| Archetype | Pattern | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fixes that fail | Quick fix works short term, worsens long term | Adding people to a late project |
| Shifting the burden | Symptomatic fix atrophies fundamental capability | Contractors instead of internal skill |
| Limits to growth | Reinforcing engine hits a constraint | Growth stalls not because the engine broke |
| Tragedy of the commons | Shared resource depleted by rational local use | Platform team consumed by every team's urgent request |
| Success to the successful | Resources flow to the current winner regardless of merit | Budget follows last year's budget |
| Escalation | Two parties respond to each other, both lose | Two departments each adding approval gates |
| Eroding goals | Standards lowered to match performance | The four-hour target quietly becomes six |

**Health care:** emergency department crowding is not caused by slow triage; it
is a flow problem in which ward discharge timing sets the ceiling on admission.
Optimizing the front door while the back door is blocked is a textbook fix that
fails. **Software:** "we need more developers" against a codebase whose review
and deployment path is the constraint is shifting the burden — added developers
increase contention on the constraint. **Executive:** annual budget cycles
create a reinforcing loop where units spend to protect next year's allocation.
The behavior is rational; the rule produces it, and changing the rule is higher
leverage than exhorting restraint.

See [topics/systems-thinking](topics/systems-thinking/).


### Sociotechnical systems and Conway's law

Two structural findings that predict a great deal of organizational behavior.

**Sociotechnical systems.** From the Tavistock Institute's 1950s coal mining
studies: a new mechanized method raised technical efficiency, destroyed the
small self-managing teams the old method depended on, and productivity fell. The
principle is **joint optimization** — the social and technical systems must be
designed together, or optimizing either alone degrades the whole.

Related principles: *minimal critical specification* (specify no more than
necessary), *variance control at source* (handle problems where they arise),
*multifunctional workers* (redundancy of function beats redundancy of parts),
and *boundary location* (draw team boundaries around whole tasks).

Every digital transformation is a sociotechnical intervention whether or not it
is framed as one. Electronic health records, CI/CD pipelines, workflow tools,
and AI assistants all reallocate authority, attention, and status along with
tasks. Deployments that treat this as a training problem fail predictably.

**Conway's law.** Melvin Conway, 1967: "Organizations which design systems are
constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures
of these organizations."

The system you build mirrors how your teams talk. The **inverse Conway
maneuver** exploits this: choose the architecture you want, then organize teams
to match. A reorganization is an architecture decision whether or not anyone in
the room knows it.

Signals Conway's law is operating: a module no team owns; an API shaped like a
reporting line rather than a domain; a coordination layer that exists only to
bridge two org units; integration bugs concentrated at team boundaries.

**Health care:** a patient pathway crossing four departments produces four
records, four handovers, and four opportunities for information loss — the
pathway mirrors the departments, not the patient. Integrated care teams are an
inverse Conway maneuver on clinical services. **Software:** microservice
boundaries that do not match team boundaries produce either constant cross-team
coordination or de facto shared ownership. **Executive:** a product customers
experience as fragmented was built by units that do not talk; adding a "customer
experience" function adds a fifth silo.

See [topics/sociotechnical-systems](topics/sociotechnical-systems/) and
[topics/conways-law](topics/conways-law/).


## Part 2: The OD process

### The cycle

OD is a cycle, not a project. Each intervention changes the system and therefore
invalidates part of the original diagnosis.

```
Entry → Contracting → Diagnosis → Feedback → Planning →
Intervention → Evaluation → Institutionalize or exit
                    ↑                              |
                    └──────────────────────────────┘
```

Underneath sits **action research**, Kurt Lewin's method: knowledge about a
social system is produced by trying to change it, with the members of the system
as co-researchers rather than as subjects. His summary: "No action without
research; no research without action."

Participation is not politeness; it is a method claim. People in the system hold
data no outsider can obtain. People implement what they helped design. And the
act of collecting and discussing data is itself an intervention — which is why
"let's just do a survey first" is never neutral.

See [topics/od-process-cycle](topics/od-process-cycle/) and
[topics/action-research](topics/action-research/).


### Entry and contracting

Contracting is the stage practitioners most often skip and most often regret
skipping.

**Do not accept the presenting problem at face value.** "We need team building"
usually means "two directors are at war." "We need a new tool" usually means
"our process is undefined and we hope the tool will define it." Write down the
presenting problem verbatim; state your own hypothesis separately; revisit both
after diagnosis.

A contract must settle:

* **Sponsor vs. client system.** The sponsor pays and authorizes; the client
  system is the group whose behavior must change. If the sponsor is not in the
  client system, the diagnosis will eventually implicate someone who never
  agreed to be examined.
* **Scope and boundaries**, and what happens when diagnosis points outside the
  boundary. It usually does.
* **Access**: who you may interview, what data you may see, what you may observe.
* **Confidentiality**: what is attributable, what is aggregated, what the
  sponsor will and will not be told.
* **Decision rights**: who decides which interventions proceed. If the answer is
  "we'll see", the answer is "nobody".
* **Your role**: expert, pair of hands, or collaborator (Peter Block's three
  modes). Most OD work should be collaborative; most clients initially want a
  pair of hands; most practitioners default to expert.
* **Success criteria** and **exit conditions**, including the condition that the
  work is not working.

Say the wants and offers out loud. What I want from you: access, time, candor, a
decision forum, an executive who will act on unwelcome findings. What you can
expect from me: a diagnosis you may not like, feedback to participants before
leadership, no surprises in public, and an honest statement when an intervention
has failed.

**Red flags at entry:** the sponsor names the intervention before the diagnosis;
the sponsor excludes themselves from scope; a specific conclusion is
off-limits; the real decision-maker will not meet you; the timeline is set by an
announcement already made; you are being hired to validate a decision already
taken.

**Health care:** name the clinical governance route, the information governance
rules for patient data, and the fact that patient-safety findings cannot be
withheld — before you start. **Software:** settle whether you may read the issue
tracker, the deployment telemetry, and the incident history; this data is
usually more honest than the interviews. **Executive:** when the client system
is the executive team, the contract must state that the CEO's own behavior is in
scope, and how feedback to the CEO will be delivered. Agree this before you hear
anything.

See [topics/entry-and-contracting](topics/entry-and-contracting/).


### Diagnosis and data gathering

Diagnosis has two parts: choosing a frame (see
[Part 3](#part-3-diagnostic-frameworks)) and gathering data to populate it.

**Methods, with their biases:**

| Method | Strength | Bias to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Individual interviews | Depth, candor, surfaces the undiscussable | Recency; articulate people dominate; you hear what people think you want |
| Focus groups | Efficient; surfaces shared norms | Seniority silences; consensus effects |
| Surveys | Scale, comparability, trend | Perception not behavior; response bias; low action rate |
| Direct observation | What people actually do | Observer effect; sampling; time cost |
| Documents and artifacts | Formal system as designed | Describes intent, not practice |
| System telemetry | Objective, continuous, hard to game | Measures only what is instrumented |
| Network mapping | Reveals the informal organization | Sensitive; consent required |

**Triangulate.** Any finding that appears in only one method is a hypothesis.
Findings that appear in interviews, in observation, and in telemetry are
findings.

**Interview practice.** Twelve to twenty interviews usually reaches saturation
in a single unit — you stop hearing new things. Open with an explicit
confidentiality statement including its exceptions. Ask for stories and
specifics, not opinions: "tell me about the last time that happened" beats "what
do you think of our communication?" Ask what surprised them in their first
month. Ask what they would tell a friend considering joining. Ask what everyone
knows and nobody says.

**The best free instrument available:** ask people in week three of their
employment what surprised them. They can still see the culture, and they have
not yet learned not to mention it.

**Observation practice.** Watch the actual work. Count things: who speaks and
for how long; how many times a task is interrupted; how long work waits between
steps. Counting converts an impression into a finding that survives challenge.

**Health care:** observe a drug round, a handover, and a ward round before
forming any view. Clinicians will accept observational data about their own
process and will reject an opinion. **Software:** read the last twenty incident
reviews and the pull request history before interviewing anyone; you will know
where the friction is. **Executive:** attend three executive meetings as a
silent observer. Record who speaks first, how long until convergence, and how
many decisions are revisited. That data set is usually the whole diagnosis.


### Feedback

Give the data back to the people it came from, **before** you give it to the
sponsor.

This is a founding OD practice with an empirical basis. The Michigan survey
feedback studies found that units where managers discussed results with their
own teams improved, and units that received the same report without discussion
did not. Collecting data changes nothing; feeding it back and working with it
does.

**The cascade.** Feed back top-down in family groups — each manager with their
own team, starting at the top. Each group sees its own data plus the aggregate
above, interprets its own data, and commits to what it will change within its
own control.

Why the sequence matters: senior leaders confront their own data first, which is
the credibility test; each manager must discuss uncomfortable results with their
own team, which is where behavior changes; and escalation is filtered, so only
genuine organization-level constraints travel upward.

Rules:

* Give each group its own data, not just the organizational average.
* The group interprets. An analyst who arrives with conclusions has taken the
  ownership that produces the change.
* Actions must be within the group's control.
* Close the loop visibly — "you said, we did", including honest refusals with
  reasons. The honest refusal preserves more trust than silence.

**The damage of doing it badly:** surveying without feedback measurably reduces
trust below the pre-survey baseline. Do this three times and you have taught
your workforce that disclosure is pointless.

See [topics/survey-feedback-method](topics/survey-feedback-method/).


### Intervention design

Convert diagnosis into hypotheses, and hypotheses into interventions with named
owners, dates, and measures.

Write a **theory of change** for anything substantial:

```
Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact
```

Build it backwards: state the impact, ask what outcomes must occur, what outputs
would produce them, what activities produce those, what inputs are required.
Then — the part that matters — **write the assumption between each pair of
boxes, and rate your confidence in it.** The assumptions are the falsifiable
content, and they are where programs fail.

Outputs are not evidence of success. "We trained 400 people" is an output.
Whether anyone behaves differently 90 days later is an outcome.

See [theory of change](#theory-of-change) and
[topics/theory-of-change](topics/theory-of-change/).


### Implementation

Three rules that govern most of the difference between programs that work and
programs that do not.

**1. Start where credibility is cheapest to earn.** A visible, small, fast win
that the sponsor did not have to force. Credibility is the currency you spend on
the difficult intervention later.

**2. Do not stack changes.** One major intervention per group at a time. Two
simultaneous transitions do not resolve faster than one; they resolve slower and
with more damage. See [Satir change model](#satir-change-model).

**3. Expect and plan for the dip.** Every change requiring new competence makes
people temporarily worse at their jobs. Reduce other demands during the dip,
shorten feedback loops, and say publicly that the dip was expected and has an
end.


### Evaluation

Compare what happened against what you predicted — including the possibility
that nothing changed or something got worse. See
[Part 8](#part-8-measurement).

Evaluation is a decision point, not a report. The decisions available are:
adopt, adapt, or abandon. A program that has no abandon condition is not being
evaluated.


### Sustaining and exit

A change is complete when a person joining next month does the new thing without
being told.

Institutionalization mechanisms, in order of durability:

1. **Selection and promotion criteria.** Who gets hired and who gets promoted is
   the strongest long-run lever, because it changes who "we" are.
2. **What leaders attend to and measure routinely.**
3. **Rewards and resource allocation.**
4. **Systems, tooling, and defaults.** Make the new way the path of least
   resistance. If the right thing is harder than the wrong thing, exhortation
   will not close the gap.
5. **Onboarding and documentation.**
6. **Stories and rituals.**

Changes that depend on one enthusiastic person are not changes; they are that
person. Before exit, ask: what happens to this if the sponsor leaves next
quarter? If the honest answer is "it stops", the work is not finished.


## Part 3: Diagnostic frameworks

### Choosing a diagnostic framework

You do not need twelve models. You need to pick one in five minutes.

| If you need to… | Use | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Get a shared diagnosis in one meeting | [Weisbord six-box](#weisbord-six-box-model) | 90 min |
| Check internal alignment during strategy execution | [McKinsey 7S](#mckinsey-7s-framework) | Half day |
| Trace cause and effect from external forces to performance | [Burke-Litwin](#burke-litwin-causal-model) | Weeks |
| Understand why good components produce bad results | [Nadler-Tushman congruence](#nadler-tushman-congruence-model) | Days |
| Make a structural or reorganization decision | [Galbraith star](#galbraith-star-model) | Days |
| Check second-order effects of a technology change | [Leavitt diamond](#leavitt-diamond-model) | 30 min |
| Assess capability level and plan progression | [Maturity models](#maturity-models) | Days |
| Understand why a change has stalled | [Force field analysis](#force-field-analysis) | 60 min |
| Scan the external environment | [PESTLE](#swot-and-pestle) | Half day |
| Decide what *kind* of problem this is | [Cynefin](#cynefin-framework) | 60 min |
| Diagnose slow response and constant escalation | [Viable system model](#viable-system-model) | Weeks |
| Find the real influence structure | [Network analysis](#organizational-network-analysis) | Weeks |
| Design knowledge-work team boundaries | [Team topologies](#team-topologies) | Days |

**A recommended default sequence:** run 7S or six-box first as a rapid screen to
locate friction. When a specific performance breakdown or deep resistance is
flagged, run Burke-Litwin to trace it upstream and determine whether the problem
is transformational or transactional. Then choose the intervention family.


### McKinsey 7S framework

Created in the late 1970s by Tom Peters and Robert Waterman with Richard Pascale
and Anthony Athos, and published in *In Search of Excellence*. An organization
is a web of seven interdependent factors that must be aligned.

**Hard elements** — explicit, visible, directly manageable:

1. **Strategy** — the plan to allocate resources and secure advantage.
2. **Structure** — hierarchy, reporting lines, how roles are grouped.
3. **Systems** — daily procedures, workflows, IT platforms, budgeting,
   performance monitoring.

**Soft elements** — abstract, culture-rooted, harder to change:

4. **Shared values** (center) — core beliefs and norms that anchor everything
   else. Originally "superordinate goals".
5. **Style** — how leadership actually behaves, not what it preaches.
6. **Staff** — the talent pool, demographics, recruitment, development.
7. **Skills** — the distinctive capabilities of the workforce and enterprise.

**Use when:** evaluating internal alignment during strategy execution or a
process-heavy transformation.

**The core insight:** there is no hierarchy among the seven. Change any one and
the other six require adjustment. Most failed change happens because leaders
pull the hard levers — restructure, announce a strategy — while ignoring the
soft ones.

**Limitations:** a static snapshot; omits the external environment entirely;
establishes no cause-and-effect ordering, so it tells you where friction is but
not what to fix first.

**Health care:** Strategy says "integrated, community-first care"; Structure is
still organized by acute specialty; Systems still count admissions; Skills and
Style are trained for acute episodes. Every S except the strategy points the old
way, which is why the strategy does not move. **Software:** a "you build it, you
run it" Strategy against a Structure with a separate operations team and Systems
that require an approval board is not an execution failure — it is a
misalignment across three S's. **Executive:** the fastest use is a leadership
team rating all seven independently and comparing. Where the executive team's
own scores diverge most is where they have not agreed on something they believe
they agreed on.

See [topics/mckinsey-7s-framework](topics/mckinsey-7s-framework/) and the
questionnaire in [Part 11](#part-11-questionnaires-and-templates).


### Burke-Litwin causal model

W. Warner Burke and George Litwin, 1992. Twelve dimensions arranged as an
input-throughput-output cascade with explicit causal direction. The most
analytically powerful diagnostic in common use.

**Input**

1. **External environment** — markets, regulation, competitors, technology.
   The ultimate trigger.

**Transformational factors** — change these and the organization's character
changes:

2. **Mission and strategy**
3. **Leadership**
4. **Organizational culture**

**Transactional factors** — everyday operational mechanisms:

5. **Structure**
6. **Management practices**
7. **Systems** (policies and procedures)
8. **Work unit climate**
9. **Task requirements and individual skills**
10. **Individual needs and values**
11. **Motivation**

**Output**

12. **Individual and organizational performance**, which feeds back to the
    environment.

**The distinction that makes this model worth the effort:**
**transformational vs. transactional.** Transformational problems require
changes to mission, leadership, or culture. Transactional problems are fixed by
adjusting structure, systems, practices, or climate.

Applying a transactional fix to a transformational problem is the most common
expensive failure in organizational life. Restructuring to fix a culture problem.
Buying a tool to fix a strategy problem. Running a training program to fix a
leadership problem. Each of these consumes a year, produces motion, changes
nothing, and burns the organization's willingness to try again.

**Motivation as mediator.** Motivation sits near the bottom as a clearinghouse:
how people experience the upstream structure, climate, and management practices
determines motivation, which determines performance. Low motivation is therefore
almost never treated at the motivation layer.

**Use when:** performance is declining and you need to find the root cause;
during deep cultural change; when previous fixes have not worked.

**Limitations:** data-heavy and slow; requires significant stakeholder
consultation; twelve interacting factors is more than most groups will hold.

**Health care:** falling performance on a pathway traced upstream typically
lands at Management practices and Work unit climate — not at Individual skills,
where retraining would have been aimed. **Software:** delivery slowdown traced
upstream usually lands at Structure and Systems (coupling, approval gates), not
at Motivation, where the engagement program would have been aimed.
**Executive:** the model's chief executive value is preventing you from
authorizing a restructure to solve a leadership problem — including when the
leadership problem is yours.

See [topics/burke-litwin-causal-model](topics/burke-litwin-causal-model/) and
the questionnaire in [Part 11](#part-11-questionnaires-and-templates).


### Weisbord six-box model

Marvin Weisbord, 1976. The fastest useful diagnostic: six boxes, one question
each, plus an environment ring.

1. **Purpose** — what business are we in? Does agreement hold under pressure?
2. **Structure** — how do we divide the work? Does it fit the purpose?
3. **Relationships** — how do we manage conflict between people, between units,
   and between people and their technology?
4. **Rewards** — is there an incentive for doing all that needs doing? What is
   actually rewarded, as opposed to stated?
5. **Leadership** — does someone keep the boxes in balance?
6. **Helpful mechanisms** — adequate coordinating technologies: planning,
   budgeting, measurement, information systems, meetings?

**The key move:** for each box, examine both the **formal system** (what the
documents say) and the **informal system** (what actually happens). The gap is
the diagnosis. A team with a documented escalation path nobody uses has a
Relationships and Leadership problem, not a documentation problem.

**Running it:** ninety minutes, mixed-level group of 8–12. One flip chart per
box, formal on the left, informal on the right. Silent writing first, then
round-robin — this prevents the loudest voice anchoring the group. Dot-vote the
box with the biggest formal/informal gap.

**Health care:** Purpose is usually strong — clinicians agree on patient care.
Rewards is usually the weak box: activity targets push against the stated
purpose, and staff feel the contradiction daily. **Software:** Helpful
mechanisms is the box most often mistaken for the problem ("we need a better
tracker") while Structure and Relationships are the real gaps. Ask what the
tracker is being asked to compensate for. **Executive:** the Leadership box asks
whether anyone is holding the balance. In many executive teams every member owns
a box and nobody owns the fit between them.

See [topics/weisbord-six-box-model](topics/weisbord-six-box-model/).


### Nadler-Tushman congruence model

David Nadler and Michael Tushman, 1980. Performance comes from the **fit**
between components, not from the quality of any one.

**Inputs:** environment, resources, history, and strategy.

**Transformation process — four components:**

1. **Work** — the tasks, their flow, their uncertainty and interdependence.
2. **People** — skills, expectations, preferences.
3. **Formal organization** — structure, processes, systems, metrics, rewards.
4. **Informal organization** — culture, norms, networks, actual power.

**Outputs** at system, unit, and individual level.

Diagnosis means examining all **six pairwise fits**: Work↔People, Work↔Formal,
Work↔Informal, People↔Formal, People↔Informal, and Formal↔Informal. The last
pair is usually where the diagnosis lands.

**The central claim:** a world-class component that does not fit the others
reduces effectiveness. This is why hiring excellent people into a badly designed
system produces frustration rather than performance.

**Health care:** Work is high-uncertainty and highly interdependent; Formal
organization is designed for standardized throughput. That single misfit
explains a large share of clinician frustration and workaround behavior.
**Software:** an org adopts autonomous product teams while retaining centralized
annual funding and approval. Autonomy is announced; dependency is preserved;
teams correctly conclude the change was cosmetic. **Executive:** a strategy
shift toward long-horizon investment, against a quarterly bonus system and a
hero culture that rewards firefighting. The strategy is not wrong; it is
incongruent.

**Limitation worth stating:** congruence can mean comfortable stagnation. An
organization perfectly aligned to a market that no longer exists is perfectly
congruent. Fit is necessary, not sufficient.

See [topics/nadler-tushman-congruence-model](topics/nadler-tushman-congruence-model/).


### Galbraith star model

Jay Galbraith's framework for organization *design*. Five policy areas, all
controlled by leadership, all of which must point the same way.

1. **Strategy** — determines the design criteria. Without it, design arguments
   are unresolvable.
2. **Structure** — where power sits: specialization, shape (spans and layers),
   distribution of power, departmentalization (function, product, geography,
   customer, or matrix).
3. **Processes** — information and decision flows. *Vertical* processes
   (planning, budgeting) allocate resources; *lateral* processes (teams,
   integrator roles, networks) coordinate across units without escalation.
4. **Rewards** — goals, metrics, evaluation, compensation, promotion. The most
   commonly forgotten point, and the one that silently vetoes the others.
5. **People** — recruitment, selection, development, rotation.

**The governing rule:** change one point and you must reconsider the other four.
A structure change without matching processes, rewards, and people development
produces new boxes, identical behavior, and a claim that "the reorganization
didn't work."

**Galbraith's cheap lever:** lateral processes. Most coordination problems
presented as structural can be solved with an explicit integrator role, a
standing cross-unit forum, or a shared metric — at a fraction of the cost of a
restructure.

**Design practice:** write three to five design criteria from strategy,
including what the design is allowed to sacrifice. Generate two or three
genuinely different options. Score each, naming the tradeoff it accepts. Design
the lateral processes, rewards, and people moves **before** announcing the
structure. Name what will get worse — every design has a downside, and unnamed
downsides become surprises.

**Health care:** service line vs. professional hierarchy is a classic matrix. A
cancer service crossing surgery, oncology, radiology, and nursing works or fails
almost entirely on whether the lateral processes and rewards were designed, or
whether only the box was drawn. **Software:** moving to product-aligned teams
without changing funding, performance criteria, and career ladders means
engineers keep optimizing for their functional manager. **Executive:** the
highest-leverage question in an executive design discussion is "which dimension
gets primacy — function, product, geography, or customer?" Refusing to answer
produces a matrix in which every conflict escalates to the CEO.

See [topics/galbraith-star-model](topics/galbraith-star-model/).


### Leavitt diamond model

Harold Leavitt, 1965. The ancestor of the later models, and still the fastest
check on second-order effects. Four interacting variables: **Task**, **People**,
**Structure**, **Technology**.

For any proposed change, ask what it does to the other three.

Leavitt's own point: change agents choose a favorite point of entry —
technologists change technology, HR changes people, executives change structure
— and are then surprised by effects at the other three.

**Health care:** adding a digital triage tool shifts decision authority from
experienced nurses to an algorithm (Structure), devalues a skill built over
years (People), and changes what triage means (Task). All four move; one was
planned. **Software:** adopting infrastructure as code changes who can deploy
and makes some operations roles obsolete while creating new ones.
**Executive:** a shared services consolidation is presented as a Structure and
cost change; it is also a Task change for every unit that had local support, and
those units will invent shadow capability.

Use it as a 30-minute checklist, not as a serious diagnostic. It has no
environment term.

See [topics/leavitt-diamond-model](topics/leavitt-diamond-model/).


### Maturity models

A maturity model assesses people and culture, processes and structures, and
objects and technology against a series of levels of sophistication.

Typical levels:

* Level 0: Not applicable / none
* Level 1: Initiate, investigate, informal, individual
* Level 2: Develop, describe, duplicate, departmental
* Level 3: Standardize, specify, scale, service-oriented
* Level 4: Manage, measure, mainstream, mission-oriented
* Level 5: Orchestrate, optimize, originate, opportunity-oriented

Well-known examples: CMMI, P3M3, TOGAF maturity, agile fluency, the Dreyfus
model of skill acquisition, the DDaT capability framework.

**Two design approaches.** *Top-down*: fix the levels first, then find
characteristics that fit them. *Bottom-up*: identify characteristics first, then
cluster them into levels. Bottom-up produces models that fit your organization;
top-down produces models that compare across organizations.

**Use when:** you need a shared vocabulary for current capability and a
direction of travel; when building a career framework or a capability roadmap.

**Do not use when:** the level becomes the goal. A maturity model is
*descriptive* — it describes levels and their items. It does not prescribe how a
team moves between them, and progression up levels does not reliably predict
outcomes.

**The rebuttals are worth reading.** James Bach, Michael Bolton, and Barry
O'Reilly have argued that maturity models impose a single linear notion of
"better", reward conformance over judgment, and measure the wrong thing.
The steelman: a maturity model is a communication device for a capability
conversation, and it fails when treated as a scorecard tied to funding.

**Health care:** clinical competency frameworks and improvement maturity
assessments are pervasive; their risk is level-chasing for accreditation rather
than capability. **Software:** engineering maturity models are useful for
identifying missing practices and dangerous when used to rank teams. Prefer
[DORA](#domain-metric-sets) for delivery capability, which measures outcomes
rather than practice conformance. **Executive:** use maturity models to allocate
investment across a portfolio, and never to compare units with different
constraints.

See [topics/maturity-models](topics/maturity-models/) and
<https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/maturity-models>.


### Force field analysis

Kurt Lewin's model of the current state as a **quasi-stationary equilibrium**:
a balance between driving forces pushing toward change and restraining forces
holding the status quo. The apparent stability is a stalemate.

**The central insight:** most change efforts add driving force — more urgency,
more communication, more incentives, more executive pressure. Removing
restraining forces is usually more effective, because added driving force raises
tension and provokes counter-pressure. Push harder and you get compliance plus
resentment; remove the obstacle and you get movement.

**How to run it:** state the target state; list and rate driving forces 1–5;
list and rate restraining forces; draw opposing arrows sized by rating; for each
restraining force ask whether it can be removed, weakened, or converted; only
then consider adding driving force. Do this **with** the people who will be
restrained, not about them — the restraining forces list written by leadership
alone is nearly always wrong.

**Typical restraining forces:** loss of status, autonomy, or identity; a reward
system that pays for the old behavior; genuine capability gaps nobody will
admit; prior failed changes; physical and technical constraints; and **workload
with no slack**. The last is the most common and the least often addressed.
People are not resisting; they have no capacity.

**Health care:** hand hygiene compliance. Driving: audits, posters, professional
duty. Restraining: dispensers positioned wrong, gloves slower, skin damage,
understaffed shifts. Moving the dispenser beats another poster. **Software:**
trunk-based development. Driving: faster delivery. Restraining: a two-hour test
suite, a manual approval board, and one person who owns release and likes owning
it. Address the test suite first. **Executive:** a return-to-office mandate.
Driving forces are asserted (collaboration, culture); restraining forces are
concrete (commute cost, caregiving, desk shortage, teams hired remote).
Mandates that ignore concrete restraints produce attrition rather than presence.

Assign every restraining force a named owner and a date, or the analysis is
decorative.

See [topics/force-field-analysis](topics/force-field-analysis/).


### SWOT and PESTLE

**SWOT** sorts factors into Strengths and Weaknesses (internal), Opportunities
and Threats (external). It is the most widely used and most widely abused
strategy tool, because it produces four unranked lists and no decision.

Make it useful with the **TOWS** step — convert the grid into options:

| Pairing | Type | Question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S + O | Attack | How do we use a strength to seize an opportunity? |
| S + T | Defend | How do we use a strength to neutralize a threat? |
| W + O | Build | What weakness must we fix to take this opportunity? |
| W + T | Avoid | Where are we exposed and weak? What do we exit? |

Discipline rules: cap each quadrant at five forced-ranked items; require
evidence and a comparison point for every item; hold the internal/external line
firmly; time-stamp it.

**PESTLE** scans the macro-environment: **P**olitical, **E**conomic,
**S**ocial, **T**echnological, **L**egal, **E**nvironmental. It supplies the
external input that 7S omits and Burke-Litwin requires.

Make it decision-grade by adding columns for direction and horizon, impact 1–5,
confidence 1–5, and owner. Then plot impact against confidence: high impact and
high confidence means plan now; **high impact and low confidence means assign a
named watcher, a specific indicator, and a threshold that triggers action.**
That cell is what earns the exercise.

**Health care:** Political and Legal dominate — funding settlements, workforce
regulation, inspection regimes. Social factors like an ageing population have a
decade-long, highly predictable impact that is routinely under-planned.
**Software:** Technological and Legal dominate — AI capability shifts, data
protection, accessibility law, sector security rules. **Executive:** use PESTLE
to force the board past the current quarter, and specifically to name which
external factors would invalidate the strategy. A strategy with no stated
invalidation condition is a wish.

See [topics/swot-analysis](topics/swot-analysis/) and
[topics/pestle-analysis](topics/pestle-analysis/).


### Cynefin framework

Dave Snowden's sense-making framework helps a group decide what *kind* of
situation they are in, and therefore what response is appropriate.

| Domain | Cause and effect | Response | Practice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clear** | Obvious to all | Sense — categorize — respond | Best practice |
| **Complicated** | Knowable with expertise | Sense — analyze — respond | Good practice |
| **Complex** | Coherent only in retrospect | Probe — sense — respond | Emergent practice |
| **Chaotic** | None discernible | Act — sense — respond | Novel practice |
| **Confused** | You don't know which domain | Break down and reassign | — |

**The central lesson for OD:** culture change, leadership behavior, and most
organizational change are **Complex**, and are routinely managed as if they were
**Complicated**.

That mismatch produces detailed multi-year change plans, business cases
promising specific culture scores, consultants selling a known answer, and
programs that cannot adapt because the plan is the deliverable. The correct
response in a complex domain is a portfolio of small, parallel, safe-to-fail
experiments with fast feedback.

**Safe-to-fail experiment design** requires, in advance: a coherent hypothesis;
a way to tell early whether to amplify or dampen; a stop condition and a way to
reverse; and a blast radius small enough that failure is survivable.

**Health care:** sepsis protocol compliance is Complicated — analyzable, with a
right answer. Improving team communication across a merged department is Complex
— try three approaches on three wards and keep what emerges. **Software:** a
production outage begins Chaotic, becomes Complicated during root cause
analysis, and the prevention work is Complex. Treating the whole incident as one
domain is why post-incident actions often fail to stick. **Executive:** a
merger's legal and financial integration is Complicated; the cultural
integration is Complex. Running both under one program plan guarantees the
cultural half is judged by the wrong criteria and declared complete when it is
not.

See [topics/cynefin-framework](topics/cynefin-framework/).


### Viable system model

Stafford Beer's cybernetic model specifies five functions any organization must
perform to remain viable, recursively at every level.

* **System 1 — Operations**: the units doing the primary work.
* **System 2 — Coordination**: damping oscillation between System 1 units.
  Schedules, standards, shared protocols. Anti-oscillatory, not commanding.
* **System 3 — Control**: day-to-day management of the whole; resource
  allocation; the inside and now.
* **System 3\* — Audit**: sporadic direct inspection that bypasses the normal
  reporting line.
* **System 4 — Intelligence**: outside and future. Environment scanning,
  strategy, adaptation.
* **System 5 — Policy**: identity, purpose, values, and balancing System 3
  (now) against System 4 (future).

**Requisite variety** (Ashby's law): only variety can absorb variety.
Organizations fail either by attenuating variety badly (ignoring signals) or by
trying to handle all of it centrally (overwhelming the center).

Diagnostic questions: is there a real System 2, or do units coordinate by
escalating to a manager? Does System 3 dominate System 4? Does System 3\* exist,
or does leadership see only filtered reports? Has System 5 collapsed into
System 3?

**Health care:** System 4 is usually weakest — service development crowded out
by operational pressure. System 3\* exists as audit and inspection but is
experienced as punitive, which corrupts the channel. **Software:** platform
teams are System 2 when they provide standards that reduce friction, and become
bottlenecks when misused as System 3 control. Naming which is intended settles a
lot of arguments. **Executive:** a board that spends every meeting on this
quarter's numbers has collapsed System 5 into System 3. Nobody is holding
identity or the now/future balance.

See [topics/viable-system-model](topics/viable-system-model/).


### Organizational network analysis

ONA maps the informal structure: who actually goes to whom for information,
advice, decisions, and trust.

Different questions produce different networks. The **trust network** — "who
would you tell about a mistake before it became public?" — is usually the
smallest, the least like the org chart, and the most predictive of whether a
change will take hold.

Roles the map reveals: **central connectors** (often overloaded, often not
senior, often invisible to leadership); **brokers** who connect otherwise
separate clusters and whose departure disconnects the organization;
**peripheral nodes**; **silos** with dense internal and sparse external ties;
and **bottlenecks**.

**Method:** prefer survey-based with explicit consent over passive analysis of
email, chat, and calendar data. Passive ONA without informed consent is
surveillance and will be experienced as such the moment it becomes known.

**Ethics rules, non-negotiable:** informed consent with genuine opt-out; never
report individual-level maps to managers about their reports; report roles and
patterns, not named rankings; never use ONA output in performance, promotion, or
redundancy decisions.

**Health care:** ONA reliably finds that a small number of senior nurses are the
real information hubs of a ward, outranking the formal hierarchy. Change
programs that recruit them succeed; programs routed only through consultants and
managers stall. **Software:** code review networks are a ready-made dataset. One
reviewer approving most changes across many repositories is simultaneously a
bus-factor risk and a delivery constraint. **Executive:** six months after an
acquisition, ONA shows whether the organizations actually merged. Two dense
clusters with three thin links means integration exists only on paper.

See [topics/organizational-network-analysis](topics/organizational-network-analysis/).


### Team topologies

Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais's design vocabulary for knowledge work, built on
Conway's law and cognitive load.

**Four team types:**

1. **Stream-aligned** — aligned to a single valuable flow of work: a product, a
   service, a patient pathway. The default; most teams should be this.
2. **Enabling** — helps stream-aligned teams acquire a capability, then leaves.
   Explicitly time-boxed. Fails when it becomes a permanent consulting group.
3. **Complicated-subsystem** — owns a part requiring deep specialism. Justified
   only when the specialism is genuinely scarce.
4. **Platform** — provides internal services that reduce cognitive load, consumed
   as self-service. A platform that gatekeeps is not a platform; it is a
   bottleneck.

**Three interaction modes:** **Collaboration** (high bandwidth, deliberately
temporary, for discovery), **X-as-a-Service** (low bandwidth, the target steady
state), and **Facilitating** (time-boxed helping). Name the mode explicitly for
each interacting pair, and name when it should change. Permanent collaboration
is a design failure, not a virtue.

**Cognitive load** is the central constraint. Minimize *extraneous* load — the
accidental complexity of tooling, environments, and process — so capacity goes
to the domain. Practical test: ask a team to list everything it is responsible
for. If they cannot enumerate it, or the list spans unrelated domains, the
boundary is wrong. Adding people does not fix an over-broad boundary.

**Health care:** an integrated care team around a pathway is stream-aligned; a
specialist radiology reporting unit is complicated-subsystem; an improvement
team that embeds for eight weeks and withdraws is enabling — and one that never
withdraws becomes a permanent dependency. **Software:** the native domain; the
frequent error is a platform team operating as a ticket queue. **Executive:**
ask of every team, which type is it and who decided? Teams with no clear type
usually have no clear owner.

See [topics/team-topologies](topics/team-topologies/).

### Choosing a change framework

| Situation | Use |
| --- | --- |
| You need the simplest correct mental model | [Lewin](#lewin-change-model) |
| Large, top-down, executive-sponsored change | [Kotter](#kotter-8-step-change-model) |
| A defined change affecting a defined population | [ADKAR](#adkar-change-management-model) |
| Assessing readiness before launch | [Beckhard-Harris](#beckhard-harris-change-equation) |
| The change is done and people are not with it | [Bridges](#bridges-transition-model) |
| Performance has dropped and leadership is panicking | [Satir](#satir-change-model) |
| Rolling a practice across many teams | [Rogers](#rogers-diffusion-of-innovations) |
| Persuasion has failed and behavior must change | [Switch](#switch-framework) |
| Auditing a well-resourced program that isn't working | [McKinsey influence](#mckinsey-influence-model) |
| You must justify or evaluate the program | [Theory of change](#theory-of-change) |
| Incremental process improvement with the people who run it | [PDSA](#plan-do-study-act) |
| Building durable improvement capability | [Improvement kata](#improvement-kata) |

**A caution on this whole section.** Change models are the weakest-evidenced
part of the field. They are useful as checklists and shared vocabulary. They are
not predictions, and a program that has "completed step 4" has completed
nothing.


### Lewin change model

**Unfreeze → Change → Refreeze.** The foundation; almost every later model
elaborates it.

**Unfreeze** creates readiness: disconfirm the current state while providing
enough psychological safety that people can face the disconfirmation without
defending. Schein's refinement matters: disconfirmation alone produces denial;
disconfirmation *plus safety* produces learning. The complementary error is
relentless bad news that makes people protect themselves.

**Change** is the transition itself. Competence is temporarily low; performance
dips. This is the stage most programs under-resource because it looks like
failure while it is working.

**Refreeze** stabilizes. Update policies, metrics, incentives, onboarding, and
stories. Without it the system reverts within about two quarters, and the
reversion is remembered as proof that change is impossible here.

**On the modern criticism** that organizations never stabilize, so refreeze is
obsolete: read "refreeze" as *institutionalize*. The requirement survives — a
new behavior must become cheaper than the old one, or it decays.

**Health care:** unfreezing a ward works when the team sees its *own* audit
data, not national averages. Refreeze happens when the behavior enters the ward
round checklist and the induction pack for rotating junior staff. **Software:**
unfreeze with the team's own delivery telemetry; refreeze by encoding the change
in the pipeline, templates, and definition of done, so the new way is the path
of least resistance. **Executive:** executives unfreeze themselves months before
the organization and then mistake their own readiness for shared readiness. The
organization is at stage 0 when the executive is at stage 3.

See [topics/lewin-change-model](topics/lewin-change-model/).


### Kotter 8-step change model

John Kotter, *Leading Change* (1996), derived from studying failures.

1. **Create a sense of urgency** — evidenced, not manufactured. Kotter's claim:
   over half of transformations fail here.
2. **Build a guiding coalition** — position power, expertise, credibility. Not a
   steering committee of delegates; if members send substitutes, you have no
   coalition.
3. **Form a strategic vision and initiatives** — explicable in five minutes.
4. **Enlist a volunteer army** — communicate relentlessly; enable opt-in.
5. **Enable action by removing barriers** — structures, skills, systems, and
   managers that block the vision. **This is where most programs quietly stop,
   because the barriers are usually owned by senior people.**
6. **Generate short-term wins** — visible, unambiguous, clearly related, within
   6–18 months.
7. **Sustain acceleration** — do not declare victory early.
8. **Institute change** — anchor in culture, hiring, promotion, succession.

Kotter's later *Accelerate* adds the **dual operating system**: the hierarchy is
good at running things and bad at changing them, so a parallel network of
volunteers runs alongside, with people in both.

**Evidence: weak.** Practitioner-derived, rarely tested as a whole. Strongly
top-down; assumes leadership knows the right answer, which is false in complex
domains.

**The steps are outcomes, not activities.** Urgency is a state of the
organization, not a launch event. "We did step 4" is the model's characteristic
failure mode.

**Health care:** step 5 is the crux. Clinicians usually agree with the vision
and are blocked by rotas, estate, IT access, and job planning. Removing those
barriers is unglamorous and is the whole job. **Software:** step 6 maps to
shipping something real — pick a win engineers would brag about, not a milestone
that appears only in a program report. **Executive:** steps 1 and 8 are
non-delegable. Urgency cannot be delegated to communications, and anchoring
cannot be delegated to HR — it happens through who gets promoted.

See [topics/kotter-8-step-change-model](topics/kotter-8-step-change-model/).


### ADKAR change management model

Jeff Hiatt's individual-level model: organizational change happens only when
individuals change.

1. **Awareness** of the need — *do I know why?*
2. **Desire** to participate — *do I want to?*
3. **Knowledge** of how to change — *do I know what to do?*
4. **Ability** to implement — *can I do it in real conditions?*
5. **Reinforcement** to sustain — *does anything keep me doing it?*

**The barrier point.** The sequence matters: find the first missing element and
address it. Interventions aimed past the barrier point are wasted.

| Barrier point | Wrong intervention | Right intervention |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Awareness | Training | Communication of why, with evidence, from a trusted source |
| Desire | More communication | Address what the person loses; involve them; work resistance individually |
| Knowledge | Exhortation | Training, documentation, job aids |
| Ability | More training | Practice, coaching, time, tooling, removing real obstacles |
| Reinforcement | Declaring success | Metrics, recognition, consequences, removing the old path |

**The two universal mistakes:** training people who lack Desire — they attend,
they pass, nothing changes; and assuming Knowledge equals Ability — knowing the
technique is not being able to do it under pressure at 3 a.m. with an
interruption.

**Health care:** a new documentation standard. Nurses have Awareness and
Knowledge; they lack Ability, because the workstation is two corridors away and
the shift has no slack. Training moves nothing; moving the workstation does.
**Software:** adopting a new testing practice. Engineers have Knowledge and lack
Desire, because the previous three mandated practices were abandoned. The
barrier is credibility, not competence. **Executive:** executives are strong on
Awareness and Desire — they chose the change — and weak on Reinforcement,
because reinforcement is boring and happens after attention has moved on.

**Evidence: weak**, and the model is silent on structural causes. A person can
have all five elements and still fail inside a system that prevents the
behavior.

See [topics/adkar-change-management-model](topics/adkar-change-management-model/)
and <https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/adkar-change-management-model>.


### Beckhard-Harris change equation

```
D × V × F > R
```

**D**issatisfaction × **V**ision × **F**irst steps must exceed **R**esistance.

Multiplication, not addition: if any one is zero, the product is zero.

| Missing | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **D = 0** | "Things are fine." The change is a solution in search of a problem. |
| **V = 0** | Everyone agrees the present is bad; nobody can describe the destination. Energy dissipates into complaint and cynicism. |
| **F = 0** | Inspiring away-day, no Monday. A compelling vision with no visible first step produces fatalism. |

**F = 0 is the most common pattern in mature organizations**, because leaders
invest heavily in D (burning platform) and V (strategy deck) and almost nothing
in F, which is unglamorous, local, and specific.

**Practical use:** score each term 1–5 with the leadership team, then separately
with a sample of frontline staff. **The gap between the two scorings is more
informative than either score** — leaders reliably rate D and V far higher,
because they have been discussing both for months.

**Decompose R** rather than treating it as a lump: loss (status, competence,
autonomy, relationships, meaning); cost (effort on top of existing workload);
risk (what happens to me if this fails); history (previous failed changes and
what happened to their champions); and **legitimate objection** — sometimes the
change is a bad idea, and this should be tested before it is treated as
resistance.

**Health care:** D is often extremely high — everyone knows the pathway is
broken — while V is contested between professions and F is absent because nobody
has authority over the first step. High D with zero F produces burnout, not
change. **Software:** engineers frequently have high D and clear F ("delete this
service, automate this gate") but no V, so you get local optimization without
direction. **Executive:** executives own V and can manufacture D. The
highest-leverage executive act is supplying F — naming the first concrete step,
resourcing it, and removing the specific obstacle.

See [topics/beckhard-harris-change-equation](topics/beckhard-harris-change-equation/).


### Bridges transition model

William Bridges distinguishes **change** (situational, external, happens on a
date) from **transition** (psychological, internal, has no date).

**Transition starts with an ending, not a beginning.**

1. **Ending, losing, letting go.** People must let go of the old way and the
   identity attached to it. Name precisely what is ending and for whom. Identify
   who loses what: status, competence, relationships, territory, routine,
   meaning, future plans. Do not denigrate the past — people built it, and
   attacking it attacks them. Mark the ending explicitly.

2. **The neutral zone.** Old gone, new not yet operational. Productivity drops,
   anxiety rises, old weaknesses resurface, and people are simultaneously
   exhausted and creative. **Say out loud that this phase exists and that it
   feels bad** — normalizing it is the single highest-value intervention
   available. Set short-range goals. Protect people from additional change. Use
   the phase for experimentation; the system is most plastic here.

3. **The new beginning.** Cannot be forced or scheduled. Communicate the four
   Ps: **Purpose**, **Picture**, **Plan**, **Part to play**.

Phases overlap, and different people are in different phases at once, including
within one team.

**Health care:** a service reconfiguration ends a team that worked together for
fifteen years. The rota is the change; the loss of a functioning team and a
professional identity is the transition. Clinicians who "resist" a clinically
sound reconfiguration are usually in the ending phase and being told they are
obstructive. **Software:** migrating off a long-lived internal system ends the
expertise of the people who knew it best. Their reluctance is loss of hard-won
mastery, not technical conservatism — name it, and give them a visible role in
the new system. **Executive:** an executive who has been through the ending
privately over months announces the beginning on day one; the organization hears
a beginning before it has had its ending, and the mismatch reads to the
executive as resistance.

**Caution:** the model can be used to pathologize legitimate disagreement.
"They're just in the neutral zone" is a way of not listening. Some resistance is
correct.

See [topics/bridges-transition-model](topics/bridges-transition-model/).


### Satir change model

Virginia Satir's model, adapted for organizations by Gerald Weinberg, is the
best available account of the **performance dip**.

1. **Late status quo** — familiar and predictable, not necessarily good.
2. **Foreign element** — something the existing model cannot accommodate. First
   reaction is to reject or minimize it. This denial defends a working model; it
   is not stupidity.
3. **Chaos** — the old model no longer works and no new model exists.
   Performance drops, sometimes below the original baseline. Normal and
   necessary.
4. **Transforming idea** — the insight that makes the new situation make sense.
   Usually arrives from inside the group, and cannot be scheduled.
5. **Practice and integration** — performance climbs, typically above the old
   baseline, but only after deliberate practice.
6. **New status quo** — until the next foreign element.

**Three predictable errors in chaos:**

* **Abandonment** — leadership sees the dip, concludes failure, reverts, one
  week before the transforming idea. The organization learns that changes get
  reversed.
* **Escalation** — pressure, targets, and monitoring added, consuming the
  capacity needed to find the transforming idea.
* **Stacking** — a second foreign element introduced during chaos because the
  calendar said so. Two chaoses do not resolve faster than one.

**Accelerating the transforming idea:** time and permission to experiment at low
cost of failure; pairing with someone who has already made the transition;
frequent surfacing of partial insights, because the transforming idea usually
appears first in one person's workaround; and protected slack, because insight
does not happen at 100% utilization.

**Health care:** an electronic health record go-live produces a documented and
substantial productivity drop for weeks to months. Organizations that plan
reduced clinic lists during chaos recover faster than those that maintain full
throughput and burn out their clinicians. **Software:** adopting a new language
or architecture makes an experienced team temporarily junior; the transforming
idea is usually a specific worked example in their own codebase, not
documentation. **Executive:** changing the operating model while simultaneously
raising targets stacks a second foreign element onto chaos. Expect a longer,
deeper dip — and expect to be told everything is fine.

See [topics/satir-change-model](topics/satir-change-model/).


### Kübler-Ross change curve

Five stages of grief — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance —
adapted by consultants into a curve of morale over time during change.

**State the caveats whenever you use it.** Kübler-Ross's stages were not
empirically validated even for grief, and she later said they were never meant
as a linear sequence. Bereavement research has largely rejected stage models.
The transfer from terminal illness to a software migration is an analogy, not a
finding.

**The real harm:** the model is easily used to dismiss legitimate objections.
Labelling a colleague "in denial" converts a substantive argument into a
psychological symptom.

**What it is good for:** building patience and empathy, and normalizing the
expectation that morale will dip. That is all.

**Better alternatives for the same job:** [Bridges](#bridges-transition-model)
for empathy with actionable guidance; [Satir](#satir-change-model) for the
performance dip; [immunity to change](#learning) for treating resistance as a
rational competing commitment.

**Health care:** staff facing a service closure are experiencing actual loss,
including of colleagues and professional identity — the closest the analogy gets
to legitimate, and the response should still be Bridges' ending work.
**Software:** a team told to migrate to a platform they consider worse are not
"in denial"; they may be right. Test the objection before psychologizing it.
**Executive:** the useful takeaway is simply that a dip is expected, has a
duration, and is not evidence that the decision was wrong — nor that it was
right.

See [topics/kubler-ross-change-curve](topics/kubler-ross-change-curve/).


### Rogers diffusion of innovations

Everett Rogers, 1962. Among the better-evidenced models here, drawn from
hundreds of empirical diffusion studies.

**Adopter categories:** innovators (~2.5%), early adopters (~13.5%), early
majority (~34%), late majority (~34%), laggards (~16%).

**Early adopters are the critical group** — respected opinion leaders whose
endorsement legitimizes. Innovators are useful for early access and useless as
proof, because the mainstream does not identify with them.

The label "laggard" is pejorative and frequently unfair. Late adopters
disproportionately include people with the most operational risk exposure and
the longest institutional memory. Interview them; they know the failure modes.

**The five attributes that predict adoption** — the strongest practical part of
the model, and a design checklist:

1. **Relative advantage** — better than what it replaces, *in the adopter's own
   terms*, not the sponsor's.
2. **Compatibility** — fits existing values, experience, and needs.
3. **Complexity** (inverse) — easy to understand and use.
4. **Trialability** — can be tried in a limited, reversible way.
5. **Observability** — results are visible to others.

Before any rollout, score your change on all five and fix the weakest.

**The chasm** (Geoffrey Moore): early adopters accept incomplete solutions for
advantage; the early majority require completeness, low risk, and support. A
rollout that succeeds with enthusiasts and stalls at scale has hit the chasm —
the answer is completeness and support, not more evangelism.

**Health care:** clinical practice change spreads through respected clinicians
and specialty networks, not management communication. Observability is often low
in clinical work, so make results visible with local data. **Software:**
internal platform adoption follows this precisely; trialability and
observability matter more than mandates, and a mandated platform with poor
relative advantage produces shadow systems. **Executive:** resist mandating at
15% adoption. Mandating before the early majority has peer evidence converts
adoption into compliance, and compliance does not produce the benefits the
business case assumed.

See [topics/rogers-diffusion-of-innovations](topics/rogers-diffusion-of-innovations/).


### Switch framework

Chip and Dan Heath's organizing metaphor: a **Rider** (rational, analytical,
easily exhausted), an **Elephant** (emotional, powerful, seeks comfort), and a
**Path** (the situation).

**Direct the Rider** — what looks like resistance is often lack of clarity.

1. **Follow the bright spots.** Find where it already works here and clone it.
2. **Script the critical moves.** Not "be more patient-centered" but "sit down
   when speaking to a patient."
3. **Point to the destination.** A vivid, concrete picture and why it is worth
   it.

**Motivate the Elephant** — what looks like laziness is often exhaustion.

4. **Find the feeling.** Knowing is not enough.
5. **Shrink the change.** Progress creates motivation more reliably than
   motivation creates progress.
6. **Grow your people.** Identity-based change outlasts incentive-based change.

**Shape the Path** — what looks like a people problem is often a situation
problem.

7. **Tweak the environment.** The highest-leverage and most-neglected tactic.
8. **Build habits.** Action triggers: "when X happens, I will do Y." Checklists
   and defaults are habits made external.
9. **Rally the herd.** Publicize what peers are already doing.

**Health care:** bright spots — study the ward with the lowest infection rate
rather than benchmarking against the national mean. Path — default order sets
and pre-filled checklists change prescribing more than education campaigns.
**Software:** scripting critical moves means a concrete definition of done, not
"improve quality"; shaping the path means paved roads, templates, and pre-commit
hooks. If the right way is harder than the wrong way, exhortation will not close
the gap. **Executive:** shrink the change — a 90-day pilot on one business unit
gets agreement that a three-year transformation cannot. Find the feeling — take
the executive team to observe the actual work, in person, once.

**Caution:** "find the feeling" is a persuasion technique and deserves ethical
scrutiny.

See [topics/switch-framework](topics/switch-framework/).


### McKinsey influence model

Four conditions, **all** of which must be present for behavior to change:

1. **Understanding and conviction** — "I know what is being asked, and it makes
   sense to me."
2. **Reinforcing formal mechanisms** — structures, processes, incentives,
   promotion criteria, budgets, tooling.
3. **Talent and skills** — practice under realistic conditions, with feedback.
4. **Role modeling** — leaders and peers visibly behaving the new way,
   especially under pressure.

**The completeness audit.** Most failing programs score high on 1 and 3 —
communication and training are the easy, purchasable interventions — and low on
2 and 4, which require leaders to change what they reward and how they behave.

| Condition | Cheap and common | Expensive and rare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understanding | Town halls, decks | Managers who can answer "why" in their own words |
| Formal mechanisms | A new policy document | Changing promotion criteria and budgets |
| Skills | An e-learning module | Coached practice with real work, over months |
| Role modeling | An executive video | An executive visibly changing behavior, including admitting a mistake |

If your program's investment is concentrated in the left column, expect
compliance without change.

**Health care:** a patient safety program with excellent training and strong
messaging fails when incident reporting still leads to blame and senior
clinicians visibly bypass the protocol when busy. Staff read the behavior, not
the poster. **Software:** a quality initiative fails when the promotion ladder
rewards shipping features and staff engineers skip tests under deadline.
Engineers are excellent at detecting the difference between stated and enacted
priorities. **Executive:** the executive's calendar and escalation behavior *is*
condition 4. If the CEO says "sustainable pace" and emails at midnight, the
organization has received an unambiguous instruction.

See [topics/mckinsey-influence-model](topics/mckinsey-influence-model/).


### Theory of change

An explicit, testable causal chain from what you do to the outcome you want,
with the assumptions named.

```
Inputs → Activities → Outputs → Outcomes → Impact
```

Build it backwards, and **write the assumption between each pair of boxes.**

**Worked example — a psychological safety program:**

| Stage | Content |
| --- | --- |
| Inputs | Facilitator time, 2 hours per team per month, exec sponsorship |
| Activities | Leader behavior coaching; team retrospectives; blameless incident reviews |
| Outputs | 12 leaders coached; 40 retrospectives; 15 blameless reviews |
| Outcomes (short) | Staff report they can raise concerns without fear |
| Outcomes (medium) | Incident and near-miss reporting rate rises |
| Outcomes (long) | Problems surface earlier; fewer escalate |
| Impact | Fewer serious harm events; lower turnover |

Assumptions, stated explicitly with confidence:

* Leaders can change behavior with coaching alone. *(Low — test early.)*
* Reported safety translates to actual speaking-up behavior. *(Medium.)*
* **Higher reporting reflects better reporting, not more incidents.**
  *(Critical — this metric moves the "wrong" way if misread. Pre-agree the
  interpretation with leadership before the data arrives.)*
* Earlier surfacing reduces harm rather than relocating it. *(Medium.)*

That third assumption is the kind that sinks programs. A rising reporting rate
is success, and someone will present it as failure.

**Health care:** mandatory in most improvement funding, and routinely produced
as compliance paperwork. Its real value is forcing agreement on whether a rising
metric is good or bad *before* it rises. **Software:** a developer experience
program whose theory stops at outputs ("we shipped a new CI system") cannot
answer whether it helped; chain it to lead time and retention. **Executive:**
ask for the theory of change and specifically the assumptions list before
approving any major program. A program that cannot name its assumptions has not
been thought through.

See [topics/theory-of-change](topics/theory-of-change/) and
<https://github.com/joelparkerhenderson/outputs-vs-outcomes>.


### Plan-Do-Study-Act

Shewhart and Deming's improvement cycle, wrapped by the **Model for
Improvement**'s three questions:

1. **What are we trying to accomplish?** A specific, measurable, time-bound aim.
2. **How will we know that a change is an improvement?** Measures agreed in
   advance.
3. **What changes can we make that will result in improvement?**

**The cycle:**

* **Plan** — state the change, who does what, where, when, and **predict the
  result**. The prediction is mandatory; without it, Study degenerates into
  rationalization.
* **Do** — run it at small scale; record what actually happened, including
  departures from plan.
* **Study** — compare result to prediction. The learning is in the difference.
* **Act** — adopt, adapt, or abandon. Then the next cycle.

**Scale of testing.** The most common error is testing too big. Ramp
deliberately: one clinician, one patient, one shift → one clinician, five
patients → three clinicians, one week → one ward, one month → spread. "Test with
one patient tomorrow" gets agreement that "pilot across the directorate" never
will.

**Three kinds of measure:** **outcome** (did the thing we care about improve?),
**process** (is the change actually happening? — usually the first thing to
check when the outcome does not move), and **balancing** (what got worse
elsewhere? — non-negotiable).

**Plot data over time, not before-and-after.** Two bars cannot distinguish
improvement from normal variation, and that is how most improvement claims are
inflated.

**Health care:** the lingua franca of clinical improvement, and most often
degraded into ritual PDSA — paperwork completed, no prediction recorded, one
cycle run, called a project. **Software:** the same logic as a hypothesis-driven
experiment; attach the prediction to the ticket. **Executive:** ask any
improvement program for its predictions, recorded before the change. Programs
that only report post-hoc successes are marketing, not learning.

See [topics/plan-do-study-act](topics/plan-do-study-act/).


### Improvement kata

Mike Rother's *Toyota Kata*: improvement capability is a **habit acquired by
deliberate practice**, not a project run by specialists.

**The improvement kata — four steps:**

1. **Understand the direction / challenge** — a longer-term aspiration set by
   leadership. "Zero avoidable harm." "Deploy on demand."
2. **Grasp the current condition** — factual, measured, at process level, by
   going to see. Most teams discover they do not know.
3. **Establish the next target condition** — how the process should *operate*,
   by a specific near date, typically one to four weeks out. A pattern of
   operation, not a result. The hardest step and the one that carries the method.
4. **Experiment toward it** — rapid cycles, one obstacle at a time. The path is
   unknown by design.

**The coaching kata — five questions**, used daily in five to twenty minutes:

1. What is the target condition?
2. What is the actual condition now?
3. What obstacles are preventing you from reaching the target condition? Which
   one are you addressing now?
4. What is your next step, and what do you expect to happen?
5. When can we go and see what we have learned from taking that step?

The purpose is to build the learner's thinking pattern. A coach who answers the
questions has destroyed the exercise.

**Health care:** a ward's challenge is "no patient waits more than 30 minutes
for analgesia"; the target condition in three weeks describes how the process
will operate, then daily experiments against obstacles. **Software:** challenge
"deploy on demand"; current condition measured; target condition "pipeline under
15 minutes for the top three services" in four weeks. **Executive:** the
executive contribution is setting the challenge, then practicing the coaching
kata with direct reports instead of asking for status. This is the hardest
behavioral change in the method and decides whether it survives.

See [topics/improvement-kata](topics/improvement-kata/).


## Part 5: Culture

### What culture is

Edgar Schein's definition:

> A pattern of shared basic assumptions that the group learned as it solved its
> problems of external adaptation and internal integration, that has worked well
> enough to be considered valid and, therefore, to be taught to new members as
> the correct way to perceive, think, and feel in relation to those problems.

Two consequences. Culture is **learned solutions that worked**, which is why it
is defended — it is the accumulated answer to real past problems. And culture
operates **below awareness**, which is why asking people to describe their
culture returns the wrong layer.

**Culture is over-diagnosed**, because it is unfalsifiable and blames nobody in
particular. Rule it in only after structure, incentives, and workload have been
excluded.


### Schein three levels

1. **Artifacts** — visible, easy to observe, hard to interpret. Office layout,
   dress, rituals, language, tools, meeting behavior, stories. You can catalog
   artifacts in a day and misread every one.

2. **Espoused beliefs and values** — stated strategies, goals, philosophies. The
   values page. What the group says it wants.

3. **Basic underlying assumptions** — unconscious, taken-for-granted, and
   non-negotiable in the sense that contradicting them produces confusion or
   anger rather than debate. **This is the culture.**

The diagnostic work is inferring level 3 from level 1, and treating the gap
between levels 2 and 3 as the finding.

**Reading the third level.** Never ask directly. Instead:

* What happens when someone makes a mistake here? Watch, do not ask.
* What gets someone promoted? Compare with the values page.
* What can be said in a meeting, and what is only said in the corridor?
* Which rules are broken routinely and without consequence?
* Who is a hero here, and what story is told about them?
* **What surprised you in your first month?** Ask new joiners in week three.

**Health care:** espoused "speak up for safety"; underlying assumption "the
consultant is not questioned." The revealing artifact: who speaks first on a
ward round, and who never speaks. **Software:** espoused "we value quality";
underlying assumption "shipping on the announced date is what counts." The
artifact: what happens in the last week before every release. **Executive:**
Schein's most useful executive observation is the persistent clash between three
occupational subcultures — the **operator** culture (the people doing the work),
the **engineering** culture (designers of systems, who prefer people-free
solutions), and the **executive** culture (financial abstraction, necessarily
impersonal at scale). Most enterprise dysfunction is this three-way
misunderstanding, not a missing values statement.

See [topics/schein-model-of-organizational-culture](topics/schein-model-of-organizational-culture/).


### Competing values framework

Cameron and Quinn's two axes — flexibility ↔ control, internal ↔ external —
produce four types. The associated OCAI instrument is among the most widely used
culture measures.

| Type | Orientation | Value drivers | Failure mode |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Clan** (flexible, internal) | Collaborate; family-like, mentoring | Commitment, development | Cosiness; conflict avoidance |
| **Adhocracy** (flexible, external) | Create; entrepreneurial, risk-taking | Innovation, agility | Chaos; no follow-through |
| **Market** (control, external) | Compete; results-driven | Share, profit, goals | Burnout; internal rivalry |
| **Hierarchy** (control, internal) | Control; structured, procedural | Efficiency, consistency | Bureaucracy; slow adaptation |

**The "competing" part is the point.** These values genuinely conflict. An
organization cannot maximize all four. Most executive teams, asked what culture
they want, describe all four — and that answer is the finding.

**Method:** respondents divide 100 points across the four types on six
dimensions, twice: **now** and **preferred**. The gap is the conversation.
Useful readings: leadership and frontline producing different "now" profiles
means leadership does not know its own organization; different units with
incompatible profiles means friction at their boundary; everyone wanting more
Clan usually signals exhaustion rather than a culture strategy.

**Health care:** typically Hierarchy-dominant for defensible reasons — safety,
regulation, standardization. Ambitions to become "more innovative" without
relaxing any control mechanism are incoherent, and staff know it. **Software:**
startups begin Adhocracy, drift to Market at scale, then add Hierarchy at
maturity. Founders experience this necessary drift as decline; naming the
intended profile at each stage defuses a lot of unproductive nostalgia.
**Executive:** after an acquisition, run OCAI on both organizations *before*
integration planning. An Adhocracy acquired by a Hierarchy will lose the people
who created the value being purchased.

See [topics/competing-values-framework](topics/competing-values-framework/).


### Westrum typology

Ron Westrum classified culture by **how information flows**, particularly
information someone would rather not hear.

| | **Pathological** | **Bureaucratic** | **Generative** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation | Power | Rule | Performance |
| Cooperation | Low | Modest | High |
| Messengers | Shot | Neglected | Trained |
| Responsibilities | Shirked | Narrow | Shared |
| Bridging | Discouraged | Tolerated | Encouraged |
| Failure leads to | Scapegoating | Justice-seeking | Inquiry |
| Novelty leads to | Crushed | Problems | Implemented |

**"Failure leads to inquiry" is the key row.** After a failure, a pathological
culture asks *who*, a bureaucratic culture asks *which rule was broken*, and a
generative culture asks *what about our system made this the reasonable thing to
do*. Only the third produces learning; the first two produce hidden information,
which is the precondition for the next, larger failure.

**Evidence: good.** The DevOps research program found Westrum generative culture
statistically predicts both software delivery performance and organizational
performance, using a six-item survey scale. This is one of very few culture
constructs with a demonstrated link to outcomes.

**The six-item measure**, rated 1–7: information is actively sought; messengers
are not punished for bad news; responsibilities are shared; cross-functional
collaboration is encouraged and rewarded; failure causes inquiry; new ideas are
welcomed. Short enough to run quarterly. Report at team level and above.

**Moving toward generative:** respond to bad news with curiosity and visible
thanks, every time, including when the news is inconvenient and the messenger is
annoying; make failure reviews blameless by design and facilitation, not by
slogan; reward the person who surfaces a problem early, publicly, in front of
peers; remove the mechanisms that punish disclosure; fund bridging roles.

One senior leader who reacts badly to bad news once can undo a year of this.
That asymmetry is the whole problem.

**Health care:** wards with high near-miss reporting are usually *safer*, not
more dangerous — establish this with the board before reporting rates rise.
**Software:** an incident review ending with "human error" is a pathological or
bureaucratic artifact. **Executive:** measure how long it takes for bad news to
reach you and how distorted it is on arrival. Ask your directs what they did not
tell you last quarter, and react well to the answer.

See [topics/westrum-organizational-culture-typology](topics/westrum-organizational-culture-typology/).


### Just culture

An approach to accountability after failure that rejects both blame culture and
blame-free culture. **Most errors are produced by systems, and individual
accountability still exists — but it attaches to behavioral choices, not to
outcomes.**

Three behaviors and three responses:

1. **Human error** — inadvertent slip, lapse, or mistake. **Console**, then fix
   the system that made the error likely and its detection unlikely. Punishing
   human error does not reduce error; it reduces reporting.
2. **At-risk behavior** — a choice that increases risk where the risk is
   unrecognized or believed justified. **Coach.** If everyone drifts the same
   way, the procedure is the problem.
3. **Reckless behavior** — conscious disregard of a substantial and
   unjustifiable risk. **Discipline.** Rare, and genuinely warranted when
   present.

**The substitution test.** Would three other similarly trained, similarly
experienced peers, in the same situation with the same information and the same
pressures, have done the same thing? If yes, the individual is not the cause.

**The outcome bias problem.** Identical decisions are judged differently
depending on how they turned out. The clinician who is interrupted and
administers correctly is invisible; the one who errs is investigated. Same
system, same behavior, different luck.

**Implementation requirements:** a published decision algorithm applied
consistently, so people can *predict* how they will be treated — predictability
is the mechanism; consistency across seniority, without which the policy is dead
and everyone knows it; separation of learning review from disciplinary process,
with different people and a clear statement of which is happening; managers
trained in the distinctions.

**Health care:** medication errors are overwhelmingly system-produced —
look-alike packaging, interruption-heavy environments, understaffing, poor
handover. Suspending the nurse removes one person and leaves the system that
will produce the next error, while ensuring it is not reported. **Software:** an
engineer deletes production data. Just culture asks why the command was
available, why it required no confirmation, why the backup was untested, and why
one person could do it alone. Firing them purchases no safety and loses the
person who now understands the failure best. **Executive:** the test is what
happens the first time a serious failure is publicly embarrassing. Every prior
statement about learning culture is settled in that moment, and the organization
will calibrate on it for years.

See [topics/just-culture](topics/just-culture/).


### Hofstede cultural dimensions

Six dimensions describing **national** cultural differences: power distance,
individualism/collectivism, motivation toward achievement (originally
masculinity/femininity), uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation, and
indulgence/restraint.

**Use it to generate hypotheses about cross-border friction. Never to
characterize an individual.**

The **ecological fallacy** is the fatal misuse: scores are national averages,
and within-country variance exceeds between-country variance on most dimensions.
Applying a national score to the person in front of you is stereotyping with a
citation attached. Other serious criticisms: single company (IBM), single era,
mostly male professional respondents, nations treated as cultures, and scores
treated as static.

**Useful applications** are about interaction design, not about people:

* Power distance mismatch: a manager says "challenge me" and reads silence as
  disengagement, while team members read the invitation as a test. Remedy:
  structured input — written first, round-robin, or anonymous.
* Uncertainty avoidance mismatch: one group wants a specification, the other
  wants to prototype. Both are competent. Remedy: agree explicitly what is fixed
  and what is open, in writing.

**Health care:** international recruitment is standard in many health systems,
and power distance differences directly affect willingness to challenge a senior
clinician — a patient safety issue. The remedy is structural: graded
assertiveness protocols and read-backs, which work regardless of background.
**Software:** distributed teams need explicit norms about who speaks, how
disagreement is registered, and whether silence means consent. **Executive:** in
cross-border integration, expect the acquirer's practices to be experienced very
differently than intended. Use the dimensions to generate questions to ask
locally, never as a substitute for asking.

See [topics/hofstede-cultural-dimensions](topics/hofstede-cultural-dimensions/).


### How to actually change culture

Schein's position: culture cannot be changed directly. It changes when the group
learns that a new way solves its problems better.

**Levers, in descending order of power:**

1. **What leaders pay attention to, measure, and control** — routinely, not
   occasionally.
2. **How leaders react to critical incidents and crises.** One layoff, one
   handled failure, one tolerated bad actor teaches more than a year of values
   work.
3. **How leaders allocate resources** and rewards.
4. **Deliberate role modeling and coaching.**
5. **Criteria for recruitment, promotion, and exit** — the strongest long-run
   lever, because it changes who "we" are.
6. Secondary: structure, systems, rituals, physical space, stories, formal
   statements.

**Formal values statements are the weakest mechanism available, and they are
where most organizations start.** A values statement that contradicts observed
behavior is worse than no statement, because it documents the hypocrisy and
gives every cynic a citation.

**A practical culture change sequence:**

1. Establish what the underlying assumptions actually are, by observation and by
   asking new joiners.
2. Identify the specific behaviors that would differ if the assumption changed.
   Behaviors, not attitudes.
3. Find the bright spots — where the desired behavior already happens here — and
   study the conditions that permit it.
4. Change what is measured, rewarded, and promoted so the new behavior is
   cheaper than the old.
5. Have leaders visibly do the new behavior under pressure, and visibly handle
   one uncomfortable case well.
6. Remove the structural obstacles that make the old behavior rational.
7. Only then talk about it — and describe what changed, not what you aspire to.

Timescale: years, not quarters. Anyone promising a culture change in two
quarters is selling something.


## Part 6: Teams and individuals

### Psychological safety

Amy Edmondson's definition: **a shared belief held by members of a team that the
team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking.** People believe they can speak up
with questions, concerns, mistakes, and half-formed ideas without being
humiliated, punished, or written off.

This is the best-evidenced construct in this guide.

**What it is not:**

* **Not niceness.** Safe teams often have more open disagreement, not less.
* **Not lowered standards.** Edmondson's 2×2 pairs safety with accountability:

| | Low accountability | High accountability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **High safety** | Comfort zone | **Learning and high performance** |
| **Low safety** | Apathy zone | Anxiety zone |

Safety without standards is the failure mode critics attack; standards without
safety is the failure mode most organizations actually have.

* **Not an individual trait.** It is a property of a group and varies sharply
  between teams in the same organization — which is good news, because a local
  leader can change it without waiting for the enterprise.

**The counterintuitive finding.** Edmondson's original hospital study expected
better teams to report *fewer* errors. They reported *more*, because they were
willing to. Reporting rate measures willingness to disclose, not underlying
error rate. See [the reporting-rate paradox](#the-reporting-rate-paradox).

**Measuring it.** Edmondson's seven-item scale, 1–7 (items 1, 3, 5 reversed):

1. If you make a mistake on this team, it is often held against you.
2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues.
3. People on this team sometimes reject others for being different.
4. It is safe to take a risk on this team.
5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help.
6. No one on this team would deliberately act in a way that undermines my
   efforts.
7. Working with members of this team, my unique skills and talents are valued
   and utilized.

Behavioral indicators are harder to fake than a survey: who speaks in meetings
and in what proportion (measure it once — the result is usually startling);
whether anyone asks a basic question in front of senior people; time between a
problem being known by one person and known by the team; whether "I don't know"
and "I was wrong" are ever said by senior people.

**Building it — Edmondson's leader toolkit:**

1. **Frame the work** as learning or as complex, not as execution of a known
   routine. If people believe the answer is known, not knowing becomes shameful.
2. **Acknowledge your own fallibility**, specifically rather than ritually.
3. **Model curiosity.** Ask real questions and wait. Silence after a question is
   the leader's tool; filling it is the leader's most common mistake.
4. **Respond productively.** The response to the first person who raises
   something uncomfortable sets the norm for everyone watching.

Structural supports: round-robins, written-first meeting formats, pre-mortems,
blameless reviews, and anonymous channels as a starting ramp rather than a
destination.

**Health care:** a nurse or junior doctor questioning a senior clinician's drug
dose. Structural aids — read-back, graded assertiveness, checklists with a
designated challenge point — work even where the culture has not yet changed.
**Software:** an engineer saying "I don't understand this design" in review, or
reporting an outage they caused within minutes rather than hours; the measurable
proxy is time-to-disclosure during incidents. **Executive:** the hardest room.
An executive who says "here's what I got wrong last quarter" buys more safety
than any program; one who visibly punishes a bad-news bearer removes it for
everyone in the room and everyone they tell afterwards.

See [topics/psychological-safety](topics/psychological-safety/).


### What makes teams effective

Google's **Project Aristotle** studied 180 teams over two years. Its most useful
contribution is the null result.

**Did not predict effectiveness:** individual ability or seniority; personality
mix or introvert/extrovert balance; colocation; tenure, team size, or team age;
whether members socialized outside work.

A great deal of team intervention money is spent on factors that showed no
effect.

**The five factors that did**, in descending importance:

1. **Psychological safety** — by a wide margin.
2. **Dependability** — can we count on each other to do high-quality work on
   time? The most neglected in favor of the first. Safety without dependability
   produces comfort, not performance.
3. **Structure and clarity** — clear goals, roles, and execution plans.
4. **Meaning** — is the work personally important to each member?
5. **Impact** — do we believe the work matters and creates change?

**Limitations:** one company, one sector; the null result on individual ability
may reflect restricted range, since everyone hired was already strong; and
popular retellings compress it to "psychological safety is all that matters",
dropping four of the five factors.

**Health care:** structure and clarity is frequently the binding constraint in
multidisciplinary teams, where role boundaries are contested and nobody wants to
name it. Meaning and impact are usually strong, which is why erosion of the
others is so demoralizing. **Software:** hiring optimizes hard for individual
ability, which the study found irrelevant to *team* effectiveness — the
implication is reallocating some hiring investment into team norms.
**Executive:** dependability is the executive-team factor most often broken —
commitments made in the room and not delivered, with no consequence.

See [topics/google-project-aristotle](topics/google-project-aristotle/).


### Team development models

**Tuckman: forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.** The most widely
taught team model and one of the least supported — derived from a literature
review of therapy groups, and later research does not find that teams reliably
proceed in this order.

Its one genuinely useful message: **early conflict in a new team is normal and
is not evidence of a wrong hire or a bad team.** That prevents real damage and
justifies the model's continued use. Beyond that, use something better.

**A practical alternative: charter instead of stage-watch.** In week one, agree:

* **Purpose** — why does this team exist, and what happens if it does not?
* **Success** — how will we know, by when?
* **Roles** — who decides what, and who must be consulted?
* **Norms** — meetings, response times, disagreement, escalation.
* **Review** — when do we revisit this? Put the date in the calendar now.

**Drexler-Sibbet** is more useful as a diagnostic, because each of its seven
stages names both the resolved and unresolved state:

| # | Stage | Question | Unresolved looks like |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Orientation | Why am I here? | Disorientation, fear |
| 2 | Trust building | Who are you? | Caution, facade, mistrust |
| 3 | Goal clarification | What are we doing? | Apathy, skepticism |
| 4 | Commitment | How will we do it? | Dependence, resistance |
| 5 | Implementation | Who does what, when? | Conflict, missed deadlines |
| 6 | High performance | Wow! | Overload, disharmony |
| 7 | Renewal | Why continue? | Boredom, burnout |

Two informative patterns: when a team disagrees about which stage is unresolved,
the disagreement is itself the finding; and a late-stage symptom very often has
an early-stage cause — missed deadlines (5) are usually unclear goals (3) or
absent commitment (4), and more project management will not fix that.

**Lencioni's five dysfunctions** — absence of trust, fear of conflict, lack of
commitment, avoidance of accountability, inattention to results — is a business
fable rather than research, and remains popular because executives recognize
themselves in it. Work bottom up: attacking inattention to results with
dashboards fails when the cause is three levels below. Its best single question
for a CEO: **"which team is your first team — the one you lead, or the one you're
a member of?"** Executives who answer "the one I lead" are describing the fifth
dysfunction, and the answer is usually honest.

**Health care:** rotating junior staff mean many clinical teams re-form every few
months. The remedy is fast explicit onboarding of norms — a written charter, an
introduction ritual, and a named person to ask. **Software:** a squad arguing
about branching strategy in week two is fine; one still arguing in month six has
a decision-rights problem, not a stage problem. **Executive:** a new executive
team's storming is expensive and public; accelerate it deliberately with a
facilitated chartering session rather than waiting for it to emerge in front of
the organization.

See [topics/tuckman-stages-of-group-development](topics/tuckman-stages-of-group-development/),
[topics/drexler-sibbet-team-performance-model](topics/drexler-sibbet-team-performance-model/),
and [topics/lencioni-five-dysfunctions-of-a-team](topics/lencioni-five-dysfunctions-of-a-team/).


### Conflict

**Thomas-Kilmann** maps five conflict modes on assertiveness and
cooperativeness:

| Mode | Stance | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Competing** | My way | Emergency; necessary unpopular decisions |
| **Accommodating** | Your way | You are wrong; it matters far more to them |
| **Avoiding** | Not now | Trivial; emotions too hot; more information needed |
| **Compromising** | Halfway | Equal power, moderate stakes, time pressure |
| **Collaborating** | Our way | Both sets of concerns matter too much for compromise |

Two observations carry most of the value. **Collaborating is not always right**
— it is expensive, and a team that collaborates on trivia exhausts itself. And
**compromising is not collaborating**: compromise splits the difference and
settles positions; collaboration digs beneath positions to interests and can
produce an option neither party brought. The tell is whether a new option
appeared.

**Interests, not positions** (Fisher and Ury): separate the people from the
problem; focus on interests, not positions; invent options for mutual gain
before deciding; insist on objective criteria. Two units fighting over headcount
(positions) may have interests in delivery certainty and career progression,
both satisfiable without the headcount.

**Health care:** a surgeon and anaesthetist disagreeing about proceeding should
not compromise — this needs collaboration if time permits, or a pre-agreed
decision rule if it does not. Splitting the difference on clinical risk is the
worst available option. **Software:** architecture disagreements are avoided
until they become expensive; use a decision record with an explicit deadline to
force the conflict into the open while it is cheap. **Executive:** executives
overuse compromising in resource allocation — proportional cuts, nobody happy,
strategy unserved. Strategy requires choices, which means someone loses. An
executive team that has never had a genuine loser has not made a strategic
choice.

**Structural aid: crew resource management.** Aviation's response to accidents
in which technically airworthy aircraft crashed because junior crew could not
effectively challenge a captain who was wrong. The transferable tool is **graded
assertiveness** — a scripted escalation ladder:

1. **Probe** — "I'm not sure that's right; can you help me understand?"
2. **Alert** — "I'm concerned about the dose."
3. **Challenge** — "I need you to stop and re-check."
4. **Emergency** — "Stop. I am not going to continue."

Scripting works because it converts an interpersonal risk into a professional
routine: the junior person does not have to invent the words or judge whether
the moment warrants confrontation. This makes it effective **even where the
culture has not yet changed**, which distinguishes it from culture-first
approaches. Related: **CUS** ("I'm Concerned, I'm Uncomfortable, this is a
Safety issue"), closed-loop communication with read-back, and structured
handover formats such as SBAR and I-PASS.

See [topics/thomas-kilmann-conflict-modes](topics/thomas-kilmann-conflict-modes/)
and [topics/crew-resource-management](topics/crew-resource-management/).


### Motivation

**Self-determination theory** is the best-supported account available. Three
innate psychological needs:

1. **Autonomy** — actions experienced as self-endorsed. Not independence; a
   person can be autonomous while following a rule they endorse.
2. **Competence** — experiencing oneself as effective and growing.
3. **Relatedness** — feeling connected to and cared about by others.

**The motivation continuum** replaces the intrinsic/extrinsic binary:
amotivation → external regulation (reward/punishment) → introjected (guilt,
ego) → identified ("this matters to me") → integrated ("this is who I am") →
intrinsic (inherent enjoyment). The goal is not to make work enjoyable, which is
often impossible; it is to move regulation toward identified and integrated — by
explaining rationale, acknowledging that a task is unpleasant, and offering
choice about how it is done.

**The undermining effect.** Tangible, expected, contingent rewards reduce
intrinsic motivation for tasks that were already interesting, and narrow
attention to the rewarded metric. Nuances that matter: the effect applies to
interesting work, not to dull algorithmic work; unexpected rewards and
*informational* praise do not undermine; and underpayment is a powerful
demotivator. Deci's practical advice — pay people well and fairly, then take
money off the table as a lever.

**Designing for the needs.** Autonomy: give rationale for unwelcome requests,
offer real choice, acknowledge negative feelings, minimize controlling language.
Competence: optimal challenge, fast specific feedback, visible progress, and
removal of obstacles that make good work impossible. Relatedness: stable teams —
churn is a relatedness tax — and managers who show genuine regard.

**Herzberg's hygiene/motivator distinction** survives as a useful heuristic even
though the two-factor structure is likely a method artifact. Hygiene factors
(pay, policy, conditions, security) remove dissatisfaction; motivators
(achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, growth) create
satisfaction. Fixing hygiene moves people from dissatisfied to neutral. His
prescription was **job enrichment** — a vertical addition of responsibility —
as opposed to **job enlargement**, which adds more tasks of the same kind and
achieves nothing.

**Maslow's hierarchy** is weakly supported as a strict ordering, and Alderfer's
ERG (existence, relatedness, growth) is a better formulation — three categories,
no strict prepotency, and a *frustration-regression* mechanism: when growth is
blocked, people intensify demands on relatedness and existence, which is why
pay and conditions disputes rise when development is frozen.

One durable implication survives: **you cannot run higher-order interventions on
people whose safety needs are active.** During restructuring, redundancy
consultation, or unsafe staffing, vision workshops and values exercises are
received as insulting, and survey scores fall regardless of intervention
quality. The right intervention is information — "we don't know yet, and we'll
tell you on the 14th" outperforms optimistic vagueness.

**Health care:** burnout research maps closely onto SDT. Clinicians rarely lack
meaning; they lack autonomy (protocols and targets imposed without rationale or
consultation) and competence satisfaction (prevented from delivering the
standard of care they were trained for). Pizza and resilience training address
neither. **Software:** autonomy over *how* is the strongest engineering
retention factor in most surveys, and competence is damaged by long feedback
loops — a two-hour build denies the sense of effectiveness dozens of times a
day. **Executive:** individual bonus schemes tied to narrow metrics predictably
produce narrow behavior. If the work is complex and interdependent, contingent
individual reward is working against the design of the organization.

See [topics/self-determination-theory](topics/self-determination-theory/),
[topics/herzberg-two-factor-theory](topics/herzberg-two-factor-theory/), and
[topics/maslow-hierarchy-of-needs](topics/maslow-hierarchy-of-needs/).


### Work design

The **job characteristics model** (Hackman and Oldham) is strongly supported and
targets the design of the work itself, which is more durable than motivating the
person.

Five core characteristics:

1. **Skill variety**
2. **Task identity** — completing a whole, identifiable piece of work with a
   visible outcome
3. **Task significance** — the work substantially affects other people
4. **Autonomy** — freedom in scheduling and determining procedures
5. **Feedback from the job itself** — direct information about performance from
   doing the work, *not* from a manager. The most commonly missed characteristic.

Variety, identity, and significance produce **experienced meaningfulness**;
autonomy produces **experienced responsibility**; feedback produces **knowledge
of results**. All three states are required.

```
MPS = ((variety + identity + significance) / 3) × autonomy × feedback
```

The multiplication is the point: **autonomy or feedback near zero collapses the
score regardless of the other three.** A meaningful job with no autonomy and no
feedback is not motivating, however important it is.

**Implementing principles:** combine tasks (reverse fragmentation); form natural
work units (give a team a coherent domain); establish client relationships
(connect the worker directly to the person they serve — powerful for both
significance and feedback); vertical loading (give planning and control that was
the manager's); open feedback channels.

**Health care:** task identity is damaged when care is fragmented across
handoffs and no clinician sees the whole journey or outcome. Named-nurse and
continuity-of-care models are direct applications, as is simply telling a team
what happened to the patients they treated. Feedback from the work is frequently
absent in acute settings, and its absence is demoralizing in a way staff
struggle to articulate. **Software:** "you build it, you run it" raises task
identity, autonomy, and feedback simultaneously. A team that writes to someone
else's specification, hands off to QA, and never sees production has MPS near
zero whatever the salary. Fast pipelines and observability are feedback
mechanisms, not just conveniences. **Executive:** when automating or
outsourcing, examine what the remaining job contains. Removing the interesting
fragments and leaving exception handling destroys skill variety and task
identity, and the resulting attrition is usually attributed to something else.

See [topics/job-characteristics-model](topics/job-characteristics-model/).


### Learning

**Single-loop vs. double-loop learning** (Argyris and Schön). Single-loop
corrects the action, leaving goals and assumptions unchanged: "we missed the
target; work harder." Double-loop questions the governing variables: "why is that
the target?"

Most organizations are competent at single-loop and structurally incapable of
double-loop, because it requires questioning things senior people have committed
to publicly.

**Espoused theory vs. theory-in-use.** What people say they believe, versus what
their behavior implies they believe. The gap is invisible to the person and
visible to everyone else. Do not ask people about their values; observe
behavior and infer the theory-in-use. Espoused: "we want challenge and debate."
Theory-in-use: "challenge is welcome from peers, not juniors, and never in front
of the board."

**The ladder of inference** — observable data → selected data → added meanings →
assumptions → conclusions → beliefs → actions, with a reflexive loop from beliefs
back to what data we select. This is why two competent people can observe the
same meeting and disagree completely about what happened. Facilitation moves:
"what did you see or hear that led you to that?"; "here is my data and the
meaning I put on it — what do you see differently?"

**Organizational defensive routines** prevent embarrassment while preventing the
organization from identifying their causes: the reorganization that avoids
naming an underperforming executive; the "communication problem" diagnosis that
avoids naming the disagreement; the consultant hired to say what employees have
said for two years; the meeting after the meeting.

**Immunity to change** (Kegan and Lahey) explains why people fail at changes
they sincerely want. The failure is a **competing commitment** protected by an
untested **big assumption**. The four-column map: (1) improvement goal;
(2) what I am doing or not doing that works against it; (3) the hidden competing
commitment; (4) the big assumption that makes column 3 feel necessary. Then
design a small, safe, reversible experiment to test the assumption.

At group level this is the highest-value use in OD, because it converts vague
cultural complaints into falsifiable claims. An executive team's column 4 might
be "we assume a visible mistake ends a career here" — which is a testable claim
about the organization, and frequently a true one, in which case the
intervention is not psychological. It is changing what actually happens to
people who are wrong.

**The learning organization** (Senge): personal mastery, mental models, shared
vision, team learning, and systems thinking. The concept fails when it stays
abstract; the operational mechanisms are blameless post-incident review with
published findings, decision records capturing context and expected
consequences, explicit predictions checked afterwards, rotation and
boundary-spanning, **protected slack**, and consequence-free channels for bad
news.

Senge's "learning disabilities" are more useful than the disciplines: "I am my
position"; "the enemy is out there"; fixation on events while blind to slow
patterns; the delusion of learning from experience, since the most consequential
decisions have consequences beyond the horizon in which we can observe them; and
**the myth of the management team** — a group that presents unity outwardly,
suppresses disagreement inwardly, and is therefore "skilled at keeping itself
from learning."

**Health care:** strong individual learning cultures — CPD, audit, M&M meetings
— coexist with weak organizational learning, because findings do not travel
between wards. The fix is structural and boring: a route by which one ward's
learning reaches the other forty, with someone accountable for it.
**Software:** post-incident reviews are the strongest learning mechanism most
engineering organizations have, and publishing them internally in full is the
differentiator. **Executive:** ask when the executive team last publicly changed
its mind. If the answer is never, the team is managing appearances.

See [topics/double-loop-learning](topics/double-loop-learning/),
[topics/immunity-to-change](topics/immunity-to-change/), and
[topics/learning-organization](topics/learning-organization/).


### Leading individuals

**Situational leadership** (Hersey and Blanchard) proposes matching leadership
style to the follower's development level *for a specific task*.

| Style | Behavior | For a follower who is… |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S1 Directing | High directive, low supportive | D1: low competence, high commitment — enthusiastic beginner |
| S2 Coaching | High directive, high supportive | D2: some competence, low commitment — disillusioned learner |
| S3 Supporting | Low directive, high supportive | D3: high competence, variable commitment |
| S4 Delegating | Low directive, low supportive | D4: high competence, high commitment |

**The two classic errors:** over-delegating to D1 and D2 — a new person given
autonomy and no structure in the name of empowerment, who flounders and is read
as a hiring mistake; and over-directing D3 and D4 — an expert supervised as
though new, which is the fastest way to lose senior people while believing you
are being helpful.

**The critical nuance: development level is per task, not per person.** A senior
clinician is D4 in clinic and D1 on a new digital system. A staff engineer is D4
in their domain and D1 leading their first team.

**Evidence: weak.** The style-matching prescriptions have repeatedly failed
confirmation. Its real value is as a **conversation structure** giving a manager
and a report language for "I need more support here" and "I need you to back off
there" — a genuine service, independent of whether the four-quadrant theory is
true. Better-evidenced neighbors: goal setting theory (specific, difficult goals
with feedback) and self-determination theory's autonomy-support mechanism.

**Health care:** clinical supervision is formally graded by competence — the gap
is on non-clinical tasks, where a consultant asked to lead a service change is
often D1 and treated as D4. **Software:** a promoted tech lead is D4 as an
engineer and D1 as a manager, and typically receives S4 delegation because they
are senior. This is the standard mechanism by which good engineers become
unsupported managers. **Executive:** newly appointed executives are D1 on the
political system of the organization whatever their track record; the first
ninety days need S2, and it is rarely offered because asking for it looks like
weakness.

See [topics/situational-leadership-model](topics/situational-leadership-model/).


## Part 7: Interventions

### The four families

Cummings and Worley's standard taxonomy.

**1. Human process** — relationships, communication, group dynamics.

Process consultation; team building and chartering; third-party conflict
resolution; coaching and mentoring; facilitation and dialogue; after action
reviews; large-group methods.

*Use when the diagnosis says:* low trust, unresolved conflict, poor meeting
quality, silence in the face of risk, unclear team purpose.

**2. Technostructural** — structure, workflow, technology, division of labor.

Structural redesign; work design and job enrichment; team boundary design;
process improvement (lean, six sigma, value stream mapping, theory of
constraints, queueing analysis); sociotechnical redesign; downsizing and
reengineering (high risk, frequently misused).

*Use when the diagnosis says:* handoff delays, unclear decision rights, work
requiring constant cross-boundary coordination, a visible bottleneck.

**3. Human resource management** — attracting, developing, retaining, rewarding.

Goal setting and performance management; reward and recognition redesign; career
frameworks; talent development and succession; wellness and workload; diversity,
equity, and inclusion.

*Use when the diagnosis says:* the system rewards the opposite of what it asks
for, people cannot see a future here, or capability gaps are real rather than
motivational.

**4. Strategic** — the organization's relationship with its environment.

Mission, vision, values; culture change; merger and acquisition integration;
organizational learning; alliances and networks; digital and AI transformation.

*Use when the diagnosis says:* the environment has changed and the organization
has not, or identity and direction are genuinely contested.


### Matching intervention to diagnosis

| Diagnosis says | Family | Typical first move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| People will not raise problems | Human process | Leader behavior change, then AAR practice |
| Work waits in queues | Technostructural | Map the flow, attack the constraint |
| Two units fight over the same decision | Technostructural | Clarify decision rights, not team building |
| Great people, bad results | Technostructural or HRM | Check work design and incentives before skills |
| Stated values contradict lived behavior | Strategic + leadership | Change what is rewarded and tolerated |
| Skills genuinely missing | HRM | Capability build with practice, not a course |
| Everything urgent, nothing finishes | Technostructural | Limit work in progress; portfolio triage |
| The same incident keeps recurring | Human process + technostructural | Blameless review, then fix the system |
| Good local units, bad overall outcome | Technostructural | Examine the boundaries and the handoffs |
| Nobody can name who decides | Technostructural | Decision rights mapping and decision records |


### Process consultation

Edgar Schein's stance, and the most under-used skill in the field. The
consultant's expertise is in **process** — how work is done, how people
interact, how decisions are made — not in content. The client keeps ownership of
the problem.

**Three models of helping:**

1. **Expert** — the client defines the problem and buys an answer. Requires
   three fragile assumptions: the client diagnosed correctly, communicated
   accurately, and the answer is implementable in their context.
2. **Doctor-patient** — the client presents symptoms; you diagnose and
   prescribe. Fails when the client will not reveal the real symptoms, accept
   the diagnosis, or take the medicine.
3. **Process consultation** — you help the client diagnose and solve their own
   problem. Slower to satisfy, more durable, and the only model that builds
   client capability.

All three are legitimate; the error is defaulting to 1 or 2 when 3 is called
for, which happens because 1 and 2 feel like value delivery.

**Principles of humble inquiry:** always try to be helpful; stay in touch with
current reality; **access your ignorance** — the most useful question is one you
genuinely do not know the answer to, and clients can always tell the difference;
**everything you do is an intervention**, so there is no neutral observation;
the client owns the problem and the solution; go with the flow; timing is
crucial; when in doubt, share the problem.

**What to watch while a group works:** who speaks, for how long, and after whom;
who is interrupted and by whom; how decisions actually get made — consensus,
default, silence, or the loudest; what topics change the energy in the room;
what is raised and dropped without response; what is discussed in the corridor
and not in the room. Feeding these back descriptively, without interpretation,
is often the entire intervention.

**Health care:** a team asks for "communication training." Observe three
handovers first, then report what you saw: conducted standing in a corridor,
interrupted four times, no structured format, no read-back. That description
does more than a course. **Software:** a team asks for an architecture review;
the observable finding is that three architecture decisions have been made and
reversed, always after the same person's objection, always outside the meeting.
**Executive:** an executive team asks for a strategy facilitator; the process
finding — that the CEO speaks first on every item and discussion converges
within ninety seconds — is more valuable than any strategy framework, and much
harder to deliver.

See [topics/process-consultation](topics/process-consultation/).


### Large-group methods

Four methods, chosen by what you need.

| Method | Size | Duration | Produces | Choose when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **World café** | 20–200+ | 2–4 hours | Shared understanding, collective insight | You want people to hear each other |
| **Open space** | 5–2000 | 1–3 days | Self-organized sessions and commitments | The answer is unknown and passion should set the agenda |
| **Appreciative inquiry** | 20–1000 | 1–4 days | Strengths-based design and commitments | The organization is exhausted by problem-focus |
| **Future search** | 60–80 | 3 days | Common ground and cross-boundary action | The issue crosses organizations and no one party can solve it |

**World café.** Tables of four to five, paper tablecloths, three rounds of 20–30
minutes on questions that build, with all but a table host rotating between
rounds, then a **harvest**. Its value is entirely in question design and
harvesting; without a named owner for each theme and a stated decision route, do
not run it. A standard progression: "what is your experience of X today?" →
"what is making X the way it is?" → "what would need to be true for X to be
different, and what is one thing you could do?"

**Open space.** No pre-set agenda; participants create it in the opening
session. Four principles (whoever comes are the right people; whenever it starts
is the right time; whatever happens is the only thing that could have; when it's
over, it's over) and the **law of two feet**: if you are neither learning nor
contributing, go elsewhere. This guarantees every session contains only people
who chose to be there. Preconditions: a real question with a real stake, genuine
openness to topics leadership did not choose, leadership present as participant
rather than chair, and a route to action.

**Appreciative inquiry.** The 4-D cycle: **Discovery** (paired appreciative
interviews about peak experiences), **Dream** (what might be), **Design**
(provocative propositions in the present tense, grounded in real examples), and
**Destiny** (self-organized action). Often preceded by **Define** — choosing the
affirmative topic, which is the most consequential decision. Topics are stated
as what you want more of, not the problem inverted: not "reduce handover
failures" but "handovers that leave the next team fully equipped."

Its serious criticism deserves stating: participants with genuine grievances can
experience AI as enforced positivity, and facilitators can use "stay
appreciative" to silence dissent. Skilled practice makes room for difficulty
inside an appreciative frame.

**Future search.** Whole system in the room — all stakeholders with
**A**uthority, **R**esources, **E**xpertise, **I**nformation, and **N**eed. Five
tasks over three days: review the past; explore external trends; **own our own
actions** ("prouds and sorries" — each stakeholder group states publicly what it
is not proud of, which does the emotional work that makes day three possible and
is what sponsors most want to cut); create ideal future scenarios; identify
common ground and plan action. Differences are recorded and explicitly **not
worked on** — groups act only on what they agree about.

**Health care:** future search suits pathway design across hospital, primary
care, social care, ambulance, commissioners, voluntary sector, and patients,
because no single organization controls the pathway. World café suits
whole-department conversations across professions and grades. **Software:**
internal open space days are frequently the most productive engineering event of
the quarter — pair with an explicit commitment session and named owners.
**Executive:** open space is valuable when the executive team genuinely does not
know the answer, and destructive when used to manufacture buy-in for a decision
already taken. Participants detect the difference immediately and remember it
for years.

See [topics/world-cafe](topics/world-cafe/),
[topics/open-space-technology](topics/open-space-technology/),
[topics/appreciative-inquiry](topics/appreciative-inquiry/), and
[topics/future-search](topics/future-search/).


### After action review

The cheapest organizational learning mechanism available, and the one most often
degraded into a status report. Four questions:

1. **What was supposed to happen?**
2. **What actually happened?**
3. **Why were there differences?**
4. **What will we do differently next time?**

Establishing the intent *before* discussing the outcome is what prevents
hindsight from rewriting what people meant to do.

**Ground rules:** no blame; everyone participates and rank is left outside;
facilitated rather than chaired, preferably not by the person who led the event;
soon after, while memory is fresh; findings recorded and shared, or the learning
stays in the room.

**Facilitation guidance:** ask the most junior people first, because seniority
anchors the account; establish the timeline factually before discussing causes;
ask "what made that the reasonable thing to do at the time?" rather than "why did
you do that?"; beware hindsight bias, since with the outcome known the correct
action looks obvious and it was not; and distinguish **counterfactuals** ("they
should have noticed the alarm") from **causes** ("the alarm was one of forty
active, and eleven were routine"). Counterfactuals feel like findings and are
not.

**Variants:** blameless postmortem (software operations), hot wash (immediate,
on-site, minutes), morbidity and mortality conference (clinical), retrospective
(agile, on a cadence), and **pre-mortem** — run *before* the event: "it is a year
from now and this failed completely; write down why." Pre-mortems surface risks
people will not raise as objections but will happily raise as fiction.

**Why AARs decay:** the senior person speaks first; it becomes a defense of
decisions already made; actions are recorded and never done; only failures are
reviewed, so the process becomes associated with blame; and the same findings
recur for years unaddressed — which is itself the most important finding and is
never named.

Countermeasure: audit your last ten AARs for repeated findings. Repetition means
the mechanism is producing documents, not change.

**Health care:** a five-minute corridor debrief after a cardiac arrest call is a
hot wash and is good practice. A full AAR for a serious incident must be
separated in time and personnel from any disciplinary process, and everyone must
be told which one they are in. **Software:** publishing post-incident reviews
internally in full — including the embarrassing parts — is the differentiator,
because teams learn from incidents they did not experience. **Executive:**
executive teams almost never run AARs on their own decisions. Reviewing a major
decision twelve months later against what was predicted at the time is
uncomfortable, cheap, and the fastest route to better executive judgment.

See [topics/after-action-review](topics/after-action-review/).


### Dosage and sequencing

* **Start where credibility is cheapest to earn.** A visible, fast win the
  sponsor did not have to force.
* **One major intervention per group at a time.**
* **Sequence structural clarity before cultural work** when decision rights are
  genuinely ambiguous. People cannot be candid about a game whose rules are
  undefined.
* **Sequence trust work before structural work** when the last three
  restructures failed. Nobody will engage honestly otherwise.
* **Do not intervene in a working team.** The urge to improve something that is
  fine is a real and expensive failure mode.

See [topics/interventions-catalog](topics/interventions-catalog/).


## Part 8: Measurement

### Principles of honest measurement

* **Baseline first**, collected before the intervention is announced, because
  announcement is itself an intervention.
* **Predict, then measure.** Write down expected direction and magnitude in
  advance. Retrospective interpretation always finds success.
* **Leading and lagging.** Lagging indicators confirm; leading indicators steer.
  Only the leading ones let you act in time.
* **Balancing measures.** What could this make worse? Every improvement can
  export harm; measure the export.
* **Plot over time, not before-and-after.** Two bars cannot distinguish a real
  shift from normal variation.
* **Aggregate, do not surveil.** Team level and above. Individual-level OD
  metrics change behavior toward the metric and destroy the data.


### What to measure

| Category | Examples | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perception | Engagement, psychological safety, Westrum, eNPS | Cheap, fast, gameable |
| Behavior | Reporting rate, speaking-up incidents, review latency | Harder to collect, much harder to fake |
| Flow | Lead time, cycle time, queue length, work in progress | Objective; needs instrumentation |
| Outcome | Harm events, defects, customer satisfaction, retention | What matters; slow, noisy, multi-causal |
| Capability | Cross-skilling, bus factor, time to onboard | Predicts resilience |
| Cost | Turnover cost, agency spend, rework, overtime | Speaks to finance; easy to overclaim |

**Behavior measures are the most under-used and the most informative.** "Did
anyone challenge a senior decision this month, and what happened to them?" is
worth ten survey items.


### Goodhart's law and metric distortion

"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Predictable distortions: **gaming** (hitting the number without the outcome —
four-hour emergency targets met by moving patients to a corridor reclassified as
a ward); **tunnel vision** (measured things improve, unmeasured things decay);
**effort substitution**; and **suppression** (reporting falls because reporting
looks bad).

Countermeasures: balanced sets rather than single numbers; pair every efficiency
measure with a quality measure; never tie individual pay to a single OD metric;
and work out in advance what the metric would look like if gamed, then watch for
that signature.


### The reporting-rate paradox

The most important measurement trap in OD.

When safety culture improves, incident and near-miss reporting **rises**,
because people are willing. When it deteriorates, reporting **falls**.

A rising reporting rate is therefore success and looks like failure.

**Agree the interpretation with the board before the data moves.** Pair
reporting rate with a severity-weighted harm measure: reports up, serious harm
flat or down, is the signature of a healthy system.

This applies beyond safety. Rising numbers of raised risks, escalated concerns,
disclosed project problems, and reported near-misses in any domain are usually
evidence that the culture work is functioning.


### Domain metric sets

**Health care.** Pair harm outcomes (lagging) with reporting culture and
staffing fill rates (leading). Use statistical process control — already
standard in clinical improvement — rather than month-on-month comparison. Always
include a balancing measure; throughput improvements frequently export harm to
readmission or to another department.

**Software: DORA metrics.** Two speed, two stability, which the research found
move **together** rather than in tension:

1. **Deployment frequency**
2. **Lead time for changes** — commit to production
3. **Change failure rate**
4. **Time to restore service**

Plus **reliability** — availability and performance against user expectations.

Why this matters to OD: the research established that **Westrum generative
culture predicts delivery performance, and delivery performance predicts
organizational performance**. This is one of very few places in the OD
literature with a quantified chain from a culture construct to a business
outcome on a large sample. It also found that formal change approval boards were
*negatively* associated with performance — an uncomfortable and well-replicated
result.

Use DORA at team level, as trend rather than target, all four together, derived
from the pipeline rather than surveyed where possible. Never rank teams on a
dashboard: different teams have genuinely different constraints.

**Knowledge work generally: the SPACE framework.** Five dimensions —
**S**atisfaction and wellbeing, **P**erformance, **A**ctivity, **C**ommunication
and collaboration, **E**fficiency and flow. The rule: **pick metrics from at
least three dimensions, and include at least one perceptual measure.** No single
number describes productivity.

Satisfaction is listed first deliberately: a productivity gain purchased by
burning people is a loss on a delay. Activity is the easiest to collect and the
most misleading in isolation.

SPACE generalizes directly beyond software. Health systems measure activity
extensively and flow-interruption almost never, which is one reason clinician
frustration is invisible in management data.

**Executive.** Demand three things of any people program: the baseline, the
prediction, and the balancing measure. Programs that cannot supply all three
have not been designed, only purchased. When asked for a single productivity
number, offer three to five spanning dimensions instead, and explain that the
single number will be optimized against.

See [topics/dora-metrics](topics/dora-metrics/),
[topics/space-framework](topics/space-framework/), and
[topics/measurement-and-metrics](topics/measurement-and-metrics/).


### Evaluating an OD program honestly

Kirkpatrick's four levels, applied to OD:

1. **Reaction** — did people find it useful? Nearly meaningless alone, and the
   only level most programs report.
2. **Learning** — did knowledge or attitudes change?
3. **Behavior** — is anyone doing anything differently, 90 days later? The level
   at which most programs are shown to have failed, which is why it is rarely
   measured.
4. **Results** — did the organizational outcome change?

Add a fifth question Kirkpatrick does not ask: **would it have changed anyway?**

Options for causal confidence, ascending:

* Pre/post in one unit — weakest, confounded by everything.
* Pre/post with a comparison unit — much better, usually feasible.
* **Staggered rollout, units acting as their own controls** — strong and
  practical, since rollouts are staggered anyway. Stagger deliberately and you
  get a natural experiment for free.
* Randomized allocation of units — strongest, occasionally possible, and worth
  arguing for when the intervention is scarce and must be rationed regardless.

**On engagement surveys specifically.** Trend beats absolute; **variance beats
mean**, because the organizational average hides that team A is at 8.5 and team
B is at 4.2, and the variance is the actionable finding; the local manager
effect dominates enterprise policy; response rate is itself data, and a falling
one usually means the last round produced nothing. Report the **action
completion rate** alongside the scores at board level — it changes behavior more
than the scores do.

See [topics/employee-engagement-surveys](topics/employee-engagement-surveys/).


## Part 9: Where to start

A compact decision aid by role and situation.

| You are… | Your situation | Start with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A clinician or ward leader | Recurring safety or quality problem | [PDSA](#plan-do-study-act) at tiny scale, then [just culture](#just-culture) and [AAR](#after-action-review) |
| A clinical director | A pathway crossing departments is failing | [Systems thinking](#systems-thinking) for the flow, then [future search](#large-group-methods) |
| A nurse or junior doctor | You cannot raise concerns safely | [Graded assertiveness](#conflict) now; [psychological safety](#psychological-safety) as the longer campaign |
| A health administrator | Staff survey results are poor | [Survey feedback](#feedback) cascade, team by team; not another corporate program |
| An engineer | Delivery is slow and nobody knows why | [DORA](#domain-metric-sets) to find the constraint, then [force field analysis](#force-field-analysis) |
| An engineering manager | The team is capable and unhappy | [Job characteristics model](#work-design) and [SDT](#motivation), before any engagement initiative |
| A tech lead | Boundaries between teams cause constant coordination | [Conway's law](#sociotechnical-systems-and-conways-law) and [team topologies](#team-topologies) |
| A CTO / CIO | A transformation has stalled | [Beckhard-Harris](#beckhard-harris-change-equation) to find the missing term, then [McKinsey influence](#mckinsey-influence-model) as an audit |
| A CEO | You suspect a culture problem | Rule out structure, incentives, and workload first via [Burke-Litwin](#burke-litwin-causal-model); then [Schein](#schein-three-levels) |
| A CHRO | You are asked for a values relaunch | [How to actually change culture](#how-to-actually-change-culture) — start with promotion criteria, not posters |
| Any executive | Two directors are in open conflict | [Interests not positions](#conflict); do not run team building |
| Any leader | You are about to reorganize | [Galbraith star](#galbraith-star-model) — design processes, rewards, and people moves before announcing structure |
| Any practitioner | You have been handed a mess with no brief | [Entry and contracting](#entry-and-contracting), properly, before anything else |

**A universal first ninety days**, if you have no better plan:

1. **Weeks 1–2**: contract explicitly. Read the last year of incident reviews,
   survey results, and board papers. Ask new joiners what surprised them.
2. **Weeks 3–5**: twelve to twenty interviews plus direct observation of the
   actual work. Count things.
3. **Week 6**: pick a diagnostic frame and populate it. Name whether the problem
   is transformational or transactional.
4. **Weeks 7–8**: feed back to participants first, in their own groups. Let them
   interpret.
5. **Weeks 9–10**: agree one intervention with a named owner, a date, a
   prediction, and a balancing measure.
6. **Weeks 11–13**: run it small. Study the result against the prediction.
   Decide: adopt, adapt, or abandon.


## Part 10: Anti-patterns

The recurring failure modes, with their tells and their remedies.

**1. Intervention before diagnosis.**
*Tell:* the sponsor names the solution at entry — "we need a reorganization", "we
need a culture programme".
*Remedy:* contract for a diagnosis phase with a decision point at the end.

**2. Solving a transformational problem with a transactional fix.**
*Tell:* the third restructure in four years; a new tool announced as the answer
to a behavioral problem.
*Remedy:* [Burke-Litwin](#burke-litwin-causal-model). Name the tier.

**3. The training reflex.**
*Tell:* every problem produces a course.
*Remedy:* [ADKAR barrier point](#adkar-change-management-model). Training only
fixes Knowledge.

**4. Surveying without acting.**
*Tell:* falling response rates; "we did this last year and nothing happened".
*Remedy:* do not survey unless you will act; cascade feedback locally; publish
the action completion rate.

**5. Consultation theater.**
*Tell:* engagement sessions scheduled after the decision was made.
*Remedy:* say plainly what is decided and what is open. People will accept
"this is decided, and here is what you can shape."

**6. Abandoning in the dip.**
*Tell:* the change is reversed at the point of maximum discomfort, then cited
forever as proof that it does not work here.
*Remedy:* [Satir](#satir-change-model). Predict the dip publicly, with a
duration, before it starts.

**7. Stacking changes.**
*Tell:* three transformations running simultaneously in one department.
*Remedy:* one major change per group. Sequence, and say what is being deferred.

**8. Values that contradict behavior.**
*Tell:* a values page nobody can recite, and promotions that reward the
opposite.
*Remedy:* change promotion criteria first. Say nothing about values until
behavior changes.

**9. Measuring the measurable instead of the important.**
*Tell:* dashboards of activity; nobody can say what outcome improved.
*Remedy:* [theory of change](#theory-of-change) with named assumptions;
outcomes, not outputs.

**10. Punishing the messenger.**
*Tell:* the last person who raised a serious problem is no longer here, and
everyone can name them.
*Remedy:* nothing works until this stops. It is the precondition for the rest.

**11. Hero dependence.**
*Tell:* the improvement stops when one person goes on leave.
*Remedy:* institutionalize — defaults, onboarding, criteria, tooling.

**12. Psychologizing a structural problem.**
*Tell:* "resistance to change" applied to people who correctly identify that the
plan will not work in their conditions.
*Remedy:* test the objection on its merits before treating it as resistance.
Sometimes the resisters are right, and that is the most valuable finding
available.

**13. Benchmark envy.**
*Tell:* "Spotify does it this way."
*Remedy:* ask what problem that organization was solving, in what context, with
what constraints — and whether they still do it.

**14. The permanent pilot.**
*Tell:* three years of pilots and no adoption decision.
*Remedy:* every pilot gets an adopt/adapt/abandon date at the start.

**15. Confusing compliance with commitment.**
*Tell:* everyone agrees in the room and nothing changes afterwards.
*Remedy:* [Lencioni's commitment principle](#team-development-models) — people
will commit to a decision they disagree with if they believe they were genuinely
heard. Mine for the disagreement.


## Part 11: Questionnaires and templates

Copy, adapt, and use. All scales are 1–5 unless stated.


### McKinsey 7S alignment questionnaire

Rate each 1 (poorly aligned) to 5 (fully aligned).

**Hard elements**

1. **Strategy** — Does our strategy clearly define our competitive advantage,
   market positioning, and growth goals? Do our daily actions reflect this
   plan? `[ ]`
2. **Structure** — Are our reporting lines, decision-making hierarchies, and
   team boundaries logical for executing our strategy without bottlenecks? `[ ]`
3. **Systems** — Do our daily operational tools, IT platforms, reward tracking,
   and formal workflows make work easier and support our objectives? `[ ]`

**Soft elements**

4. **Shared values** — Are our core values widely understood and genuinely
   lived, rather than just written on a wall? `[ ]`
5. **Style** — Do our leaders behave in a way that matches what they preach? Is
   leadership communication open, supportive, and action-oriented? `[ ]`
6. **Staff** — Do we have the right number of people with adequate support,
   onboarding, and career paths to sustain our workload? `[ ]`
7. **Skills** — Does our workforce possess the right mix of technical, digital,
   and interpersonal competencies to hit our strategic goals? `[ ]`

**Analysis.** Average the three hard S's and the four soft S's separately. A
wide gap — high strategy and structure, low shared values and style — explains
why change initiatives meet resistance. Then look for specific mismatches: a
remote-first Strategy against an old-school management Style; advanced Systems
failing for want of Skills.

Run it individually first, then compare. Divergence within the leadership team
is a finding in itself.


### Burke-Litwin causal questionnaire

Rate each 1 (strong misalignment / dysfunctional) to 5 (strong alignment /
optimal).

**Input**

1. **External environment** — How well does leadership monitor and respond to
   external shifts: market, client demand, technology, regulation,
   competitors? `[ ]`

**Transformational**

2. **Mission and strategy** — Is our purpose and strategic direction clear,
   inspiring, and understood by everyone? `[ ]`
3. **Leadership** — Do executive leaders model the behaviors, values, and
   accountability they expect from everyone else? `[ ]`
4. **Organizational culture** — Do our unwritten rules, daily norms, and shared
   values foster trust, collaboration, and high performance rather than politics
   or fear? `[ ]`

**Transactional**

5. **Structure** — Are reporting lines and functional boundaries clear, allowing
   decisions without excessive bureaucracy? `[ ]`
6. **Management practices** — Do managers allocate resources, plan workloads,
   and coordinate projects effectively to support the strategy? `[ ]`
7. **Systems** — Do our procedures, HR systems, and IT tools facilitate smooth
   workflows rather than adding friction? `[ ]`
8. **Work unit climate** — Do teams experience psychological safety,
   cooperation, and positive working relationships day to day? `[ ]`
9. **Task requirements and individual skills** — Do employees have the training,
   competencies, and tools required for their specific duties? `[ ]`
10. **Individual needs and values** — Do reward, recognition, and flexibility
    structures match what employees actually value? `[ ]`
11. **Motivation** — Are employees genuinely energized and willing to go beyond
    the minimum? `[ ]`

**Output**

12. **Performance** — Are we consistently hitting our key indicators:
    productivity, quality, retention, financial goals? `[ ]`

**Analysis.** If Performance (12) or Motivation (11) is low, trace upstream.
Determine whether the breakdown originates in transactional elements (5–10) or
transformational roots (2–4). **Do not attempt to fix a low Culture score (4)
with a tweak to Structure (5).** Transformational gaps require executive-level
intervention and years, not a process change and a quarter.


### Psychological safety scale

Rate 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Items 1, 3, and 5 are
reverse-scored.

1. If you make a mistake on this team, it is often held against you. `[ ]`
2. Members of this team are able to bring up problems and tough issues. `[ ]`
3. People on this team sometimes reject others for being different. `[ ]`
4. It is safe to take a risk on this team. `[ ]`
5. It is difficult to ask other members of this team for help. `[ ]`
6. No one on this team would deliberately act in a way that undermines my
   efforts. `[ ]`
7. Working with members of this team, my unique skills and talents are valued
   and utilized. `[ ]`

Report at team level only, with a minimum of five responses.


### Westrum culture scale

Rate 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree).

1. On my team, information is actively sought. `[ ]`
2. Messengers are not punished when they deliver news of failures or other bad
   news. `[ ]`
3. Responsibilities are shared. `[ ]`
4. Cross-functional collaboration is encouraged and rewarded. `[ ]`
5. Failure causes inquiry. `[ ]`
6. New ideas are welcomed. `[ ]`


### Change readiness check (Beckhard-Harris)

Score each 1–5, twice: once with leadership, once with a frontline sample.

* **D — Dissatisfaction.** Do people believe the current state is genuinely
  unacceptable? `[ ]`
* **V — Vision.** Can people describe the destination in their own words? `[ ]`
* **F — First steps.** Can people name a concrete first step they could take
  this week? `[ ]`
* **R — Resistance.** How large is the combined cost of loss, effort, risk, and
  history? `[ ]`

**Analysis.** Any of D, V, or F below 2 means the change will not start,
regardless of the others. Compare the leadership and frontline scores; the gap
is usually larger than the scores.


### ADKAR barrier point assessment

For a defined change and a defined population, score 1–5:

* **A — Awareness** of why the change is needed. `[ ]`
* **D — Desire** to participate and support it. `[ ]`
* **K — Knowledge** of how to change. `[ ]`
* **A — Ability** to do it under real conditions. `[ ]`
* **R — Reinforcement** that sustains it. `[ ]`

**Analysis.** The first element scoring below 3 is the barrier point. Work
there. Interventions aimed past the barrier point are wasted.

### Contracting checklist

```
[ ] Presenting problem recorded verbatim
[ ] My own hypothesis recorded separately
[ ] Sponsor identified
[ ] Client system identified, and difference from sponsor discussed
[ ] Scope in, scope out, and what happens if diagnosis points outside
[ ] Access agreed: people, meetings, documents, telemetry
[ ] Confidentiality rules agreed, including disclosure exceptions
[ ] Decision rights named: who authorizes which interventions
[ ] My role agreed: expert / pair of hands / collaborator

[ ] Success criteria agreed

```


## Glossary

* **Action research** — producing knowledge about a system by trying to change
  it, with members as co-researchers.
* **Adjourning** — the disbanding stage of a team; real loss, routinely
  unmanaged.
* **Artifacts** — the visible layer of culture: layout, rituals, language,
  tools.
* **At-risk behavior** — a choice that increases risk where the risk is
  unrecognized or believed justified. Coach, do not punish.
* **Balancing loop** — a feedback loop that resists change and seeks a goal.
* **Balancing measure** — a metric that detects harm exported elsewhere by an
  improvement.
* **Barrier point** — in ADKAR, the first missing element; where to intervene.
* **Basic underlying assumptions** — the unconscious layer of culture that
  actually determines behavior.
* **Chasm** — the gap between early adopters and the early majority.
* **Congruence** — fit between organizational components; the basis of the
  Nadler-Tushman model.
* **Conway's law** — systems mirror the communication structures of the
  organizations that design them.
* **Double-loop learning** — questioning the governing goals and assumptions,
  not just correcting the action.
* **Espoused theory** — what people say they believe, as opposed to
  theory-in-use.
* **Generative culture** — Westrum's performance-oriented type; information
  flows, failure causes inquiry.
* **Hygiene factor** — a condition whose absence causes dissatisfaction and
  whose presence does not motivate.
* **Institutionalization** — making a change survive its sponsor.
* **Inverse Conway maneuver** — organizing teams to produce a desired
  architecture.
* **Joint optimization** — designing the social and technical systems together.
* **Lagging indicator** — confirms an outcome after the fact.
* **Lateral process** — coordination across units without escalation; Galbraith's
  cheap lever.
* **Leading indicator** — moves early enough to steer by.
* **Leverage point** — a place in a system where a small change produces a large
  effect.
* **Motivating potential score** — Hackman and Oldham's multiplicative index of
  work design quality.
* **Neutral zone** — Bridges' middle phase; old gone, new not yet operational.
* **Normalization of deviance** — drift from standard becoming the new normal
  because nothing bad happened yet.
* **Presenting problem** — the problem as first stated; rarely the real one.
* **Process consultation** — helping the client diagnose and solve their own
  problem.
* **Provocative proposition** — an appreciative inquiry statement of the ideal,
  written in the present tense.
* **Psychological safety** — a shared belief that the team is safe for
  interpersonal risk-taking.
* **Quasi-stationary equilibrium** — Lewin's model of the status quo as a
  balance of opposing forces.
* **Reinforcing loop** — a feedback loop that amplifies change.
* **Requisite variety** — only variety can absorb variety (Ashby's law).
* **Restraining force** — something holding the status quo in place; usually
  cheaper to remove than to overpower.
* **Safe-to-fail experiment** — a small, reversible probe appropriate to complex
  domains.
* **Single-loop learning** — correcting the action while leaving goals and
  assumptions unchanged.
* **Sociotechnical system** — the joint social and technical arrangement of
  work.
* **Substitution test** — would three similar peers in the same situation have
  done the same thing?
* **Target condition** — in improvement kata, how a process should operate by a
  near date.
* **Theory-in-use** — what behavior implies a person actually believes.
* **Transactional factors** — structure, systems, practices, climate; changing
  them produces incremental change.
* **Transformational factors** — mission and strategy, leadership, culture;
  changing them changes the organization's character.
* **Triangulation** — confirming a finding across multiple independent methods.
